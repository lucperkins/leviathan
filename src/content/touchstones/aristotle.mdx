### Who he was

Aristotle was born in Stagira in northern Greece, studied under Plato at the
Academy for some twenty years, tutored the young Alexander of Macedon, and
returned to Athens to found his own school, the Lyceum. His surviving works
are mostly lecture notes, covering logic, physics, biology, psychology,
metaphysics, ethics, politics, rhetoric, and poetics. No single thinker before
the modern period covered so much ground, or with such influence.

That influence was indirect. Most of Aristotle's writings were lost to the
Latin West until the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, when they arrived via
Arabic and Greek translations and were absorbed into Christian theology,
above all by Thomas Aquinas. The result was *scholasticism*: a university
curriculum in which Aristotle was simply "the Philosopher," his logic the
method of every discipline, his physics and metaphysics the framework within
which theology was expounded. That is the Aristotle Hobbes met at Oxford in
the early 1600s, and the one he spent his life attacking.

### Hobbes's target: the schools, not the man

Hobbes's first mention of Aristotle in *Leviathan* comes at the end of
Chapter I, and it sets the tone. Having given his own mechanical account of
sensation, he adds:

> But the Philosophy-schooles, through all the Universities of Christendome,
> grounded upon certain Texts of Aristotle, teach another doctrine; and say,
> For the cause of Vision, that the thing seen, sendeth forth on every side a
> Visible Species … the receiving whereof into the Eye, is Seeing.

The doctrine of *species*, the idea that objects emit likenesses of
themselves that the senses or intellect receive, is for Hobbes the model case
of what he calls "insignificant speech": a phrase that sounds like an
explanation and explains nothing. Notice the phrasing, though. Hobbes blames
the *schools*, "grounded upon certain Texts of Aristotle." Throughout the book
his usual quarrel is with what the universities made of Aristotle, which he
sometimes calls "Aristotelity," rather than with Aristotle himself.

### Rejecting scholastic metaphysics

The fuller assault comes in Part IV, in the chapter "Of Darknesse from Vain
Philosophy." Hobbes's verdict there is unsparing:

> And I beleeve that scarce any thing can be more absurdly said in naturall
> Philosophy, than that which now is called Aristotles Metaphysiques; nor
> more repugnant to Government, than much of that hee hath said in his
> Politiques; nor more ignorantly, than a great part of his Ethiques.
>
> — Chapter XLVI

His objections are of a piece with his whole philosophy. Hobbes is a
materialist: everything that exists is body, and everything that happens is
motion. Scholastic metaphysics, by contrast, runs on *abstract essences* and
*separated essences*, forms that are supposed to exist apart from any
particular body, and on *incorporeal substances*, which Hobbes regards as a
contradiction in terms. He traces these notions to a misreading of ordinary
grammar: because we can say "the whiteness of the wall," the schools imagine
that whiteness is a thing that could exist on its own. Much of Hobbes's
attack on Aristotle is, at bottom, an attack on the habit of turning words
into entities.

Hobbes also rejects Aristotelian *final causes*, the explanation of natural
things by their purposes. Stones do not fall because they seek their natural
place; they fall because they are pushed. For Hobbes, explanation that does
not reduce to bodies in motion is not explanation at all.

### Rejecting Aristotle's politics

The disagreement is just as sharp on politics, and here Hobbes does aim at
Aristotle by name. Three doctrines from the *Politics* are singled out:

- **That humans are naturally political animals.** In Chapter XVII Hobbes
  grants that bees and ants, "which are therefore by Aristotle numbred
  amongst Politicall creatures," live sociably without any coercive power,
  and then spends a paragraph explaining why humans cannot. People compete
  for honour, disagree about the common good, and can use language to
  misrepresent. Political order is an artifice, not a natural growth.
- **That some are by nature fit to rule and others to serve.** Chapter XV
  attacks Aristotle's argument for natural slavery as "against reason" and
  "against experience"; the ninth law of nature requires that "every man
  acknowledge other for his Equall by Nature."
- **That the best regime is a mixed or moderate one.** Hobbes holds that
  sovereignty is indivisible, and that the classical preference for mixed
  constitutions, and the classical vocabulary of "tyranny," did more than
  anything to encourage rebellion, "by reading of these Greek, and Latine
  Authors."

### What Hobbes kept

The picture is not simply hostile. Hobbes produced an English digest of
Aristotle's *Rhetoric* in 1637, and his own treatment of the passions and of
eloquence owes it a good deal. His method of defining terms and reasoning
from them is recognisably Aristotelian in form even where the content is
reversed. What Hobbes rejects is Aristotle as an *authority*: the practice of
settling questions by citing a text instead of by reasoning from definitions.
That, he argues in the closing chapters, is what left the universities
useless for the education of subjects and left the Church free to make
scripture say whatever the schools required.
