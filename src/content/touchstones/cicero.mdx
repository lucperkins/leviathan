### Who he was

Marcus Tullius Cicero was born at Arpinum, south-east of Rome, to a family
with no senatorial ancestors, and rose by his voice. He was the greatest
advocate of the late Roman republic, consul in 63 BC, when he suppressed the
conspiracy of Catiline, and a bystander, then a casualty, of the republic's
collapse. He kept out of the war between Caesar and Pompey as long as he
could, was pardoned by Caesar, and after Caesar's murder threw himself
against Mark Antony in the *Philippics*. Antony had him proscribed; he was
killed in December 43 BC, and his head and hands were nailed up on the
speaker's platform in the Forum.

Two bodies of work survive. The speeches and letters made him the model of
Latin prose: when the humanists of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries
wanted to write Latin well, they wrote like Cicero, and every grammar-school
boy in Hobbes's England, Hobbes included, learned the language from his
pages. The philosophical works, written mostly in the enforced leisure of his
last years, are something else: *On the Republic*, *On the Laws*, *On
Duties*, *On the Nature of the Gods*, *On Divination*, the *Tusculan
Disputations*. They are not original systems but Latin surveys of the Greek
schools, Stoic, Academic, and Epicurean, argued against one another. Through
them Rome, and later Europe, received Greek philosophy, and a vocabulary
(*natura*, *ratio*, *lex*, *persona*, *res publica*) that Hobbes still uses.

### A witness on words

Hobbes's first mention of Cicero, in Chapter IV, puts him in the company of
the authorities a wise man does not defer to:

> For words are wise mens counters, they do but reckon by them: but they are
> the mony of fooles, that value them by the authority of an Aristotle, a
> Cicero, or a Thomas, or any other Doctor whatsoever, if but a man.

But Hobbes is happy to quote him when he says something useful, and Cicero
is a man who said many quotable things. In Chapter V the line that "there can
be nothing so absurd, but may be found in the books of Philosophers" is
Cicero's, from *On Divination*, and Hobbes repeats it in Chapter XLVI, with
the barb that Cicero "was one of them." In Chapter XVI, where Hobbes builds
the doctrine of persons on which the whole theory of the common-wealth rests,
the decisive piece of evidence that a *person* means a bearer of a role is a
sentence from Cicero's *On the Orator*:

> in which sence Cicero useth it where he saies, "Unus Sustineo Tres
> Personas; Mei, Adversarii, & Judicis, I beare three Persons; my own, my
> Adversaries, and the Judges;"

Cicero the lawyer also supplies Hobbes the jurist. In Chapter XXVIII, on
whether banishment is a punishment, Hobbes reports that "Cicero sayes, there
was never any such Punishment ordained in the City of Rome; but cals it a
refuge of men in danger." In Chapter XLVII the whole argument about who
profits from spiritual darkness turns on a Roman judge's question, *cui
bono*, which Hobbes has from Cicero's defence of Roscius:

> Cicero maketh honorable mention of one of the Cassii, a severe Judge
> amongst the Romans, for a custome he had, in Criminal causes, (when the
> testimony of the witnesses was not sufficient,) to ask the Accusers, Cui
> Bono; that is to say, what Profit, Honor, or other Contentment, the
> accused obtained, or expected by the Fact.

### A witness against himself

The most pointed use comes in Chapter XXIV. Hobbes has argued that property
is a creation of the sovereign: before there is a common-wealth there is no
*mine* and *thine*, only what a man can hold by force. He then calls the
republic's own champion to the stand:

> Which is so evident, that even Cicero, (a passionate defender of Liberty,)
> in a publique pleading, attributeth all Propriety to the Law Civil, "Let
> the Civill Law," saith he, "be once abandoned, or but negligently guarded,
> (not to say oppressed,) and there is nothing, that any man can be sure to
> receive from his Ancestor, or leave to his Children." And again; "Take
> away the Civill Law, and no man knows what is his own, and what another
> mans."

The quotation is from Cicero's speech for Caecina. The move is typical of
how Hobbes handles him: Cicero is worth citing precisely because he stands
for the other side, so that his admissions cost him something.

### The source of a dangerous idea

That other side is the subject of Chapter XXI. Hobbes traces the English
appetite for rebellion to a doctrine of liberty learned from books:

> In these westerne parts of the world, we are made to receive our opinions
> concerning the Institution, and Rights of Common-wealths, from Aristotle,
> Cicero, and other men, Greeks and Romanes, that living under Popular
> States, derived those Rights, not from the Principles of Nature, but
> transcribed them into their books, out of the Practice of their own
> Common-wealths, which were Popular.

Cicero and Aristotle, on this account, did not discover that monarchy is
slavery and a republic is freedom; they lived in republics and wrote down
their local prejudices as if they were natural law, "as the Grammarians
describe the Rules of Language, out of the Practise of the time." Hobbes's
own answer, that the liberty of a subject is the same under any form of
government and consists only in what the law leaves unregulated, is aimed
directly at the Ciceronian equation of freedom with self-rule. The
complaint returns in Chapter XLVI, where the doctrine "that all Government
but Popular, is Tyranny" is listed among the errors the universities took
from the ancients; though Hobbes there grants that one error, the
extension of law to men's thoughts and consciences, the schools "never
learned of Aristotle, nor Cicero, nor any other of the Heathen."

### Method against eloquence

Underneath the particular disagreements is a difference of kind. Cicero was
the great example of persuasion by eloquence, and the humanist education
built on him taught that the way to settle a question was to argue it
handsomely from both sides. Hobbes, who had that education and wrote a fine
Ciceronian Latin himself, came to regard it as the disease. In Chapter XXVI
he sets out to show "not what is Law here, and there; but what is Law; as
Plato, Aristotle, Cicero, and divers others have done," and the point of the
comparison is that he intends to do it by definition and deduction, where
they did it by authority and example. Cicero is the standard of how well
Latin can be written; Hobbes's project is to make the writing not matter.
