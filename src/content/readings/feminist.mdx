The text everyone starts from is
[Chapter 20](/chapters/20-of-dominion-paternall-and-despoticall/), which is
startling for 1651:

> If there be no Contract, the Dominion is in the Mother. For in the condition
> of Meer Nature, where there are no Matrimoniall lawes, it cannot be known who
> is the Father, unlesse it be declared by the Mother: and therefore the right
> of Dominion over the Child dependeth on her will, and is consequently hers.

Paternal right is not natural. It is a fact about how commonwealths happened
to be founded — "by the Fathers, not by the Mothers of families" — and Hobbes
backs the point with the Amazons, who contracted with the men of neighbouring
countries and kept the daughters. Set beside Filmer, for whom the father's
rule is God's own ordinance, this is a demolition.

### The sexual contract

The book that made the field is Carole Pateman's *The Sexual Contract*. Her
argument is that the social contract has a silent partner: before the brothers
agree to erect a sovereign they have already settled among themselves who has
access to women, so the individuals who contract are men, and the fraternity
that replaces the father's rule is patriarchal still. Hobbes is her most
interesting case precisely because he begins with mother-right and ends with
families headed by men — the one contract theorist who leaves the join
visible.

Susan Moller Okin had already put the question to the canon at large.
Christine Di Stefano reads the state of nature as a fantasy of men who spring
up like mushrooms, with no childhood and no debts — an image Hobbes uses in
*De Cive* — and asks what a political theory looks like when nobody in it was
ever an infant. Joanne Wright follows that line back into the seventeenth
century itself.

### The other tradition

Not everyone reads the gap as a defeat. Nancy Hirschmann takes Hobbes's
account of consent seriously enough to turn it against him: if obligation
requires a genuine act of will, most women's obligations historically fail the
test. Susanne Sreedhar argues that the retained right of self-defence gives
subjects — wives included — more than the absolutist reading allows.

### Where the argument sits

On one reading Hobbes is the theorist who dissolved patriarchal right and then
declined to notice what he had done. On another he is the honest one: he shows
that male rule rests on force and convention rather than on nature, and says
so. Both agree that
[Chapter 20](/chapters/20-of-dominion-paternall-and-despoticall/) is where the
seventeenth century comes closest to saying it aloud.
