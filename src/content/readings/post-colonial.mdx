The state of nature is usually read as a thought experiment, and Hobbes half
agrees — he says it may never have existed generally over all the world. Then
he gives an example anyway:

> For the savage people in many places of America, except the government of
> small Families, the concord whereof dependeth on naturall lust, have no
> government at all; and live at this day in that brutish manner, as I said
> before.

One sentence, and a great deal rests on it. The condition that justifies
absolute sovereignty is illustrated by actual people, in a place English
investors were then settling.

### He had a stake

Hobbes was not a distant observer of that settlement. Through the Cavendishes
he held a share in the Virginia Company and attended its meetings in the early
1620s, in the years when its policy toward the Powhatan was being decided —
the work of Noel Malcolm establishes the detail. Whatever else the American
example is, it is not something he read about only in travellers' tales.

### The colony, defined

[Chapter 24](/chapters/24-of-the-nutrition-and-procreation-of-a-common-wealth/)
puts plantations among the ways a commonwealth reproduces, and the definition
is as cool as anything in the book:

> The Procreation, or Children of a Common-wealth, are those we call
> Plantations, or Colonies; which are numbers of men sent out from the
> Common-wealth, under a Conductor, or Governour, to inhabit a Forraign
> Country, either formerly voyd of Inhabitants, or made voyd then, by warre.

[Chapter 30](/chapters/30-of-the-office-of-the-soveraign-representative/) then
makes emigration a remedy for surplus population: the poor are to be
transplanted into countries not sufficiently inhabited, where — and Hobbes
does add this — they are not to exterminate those they find, but to constrain
them to inhabit closer together and not range a great deal of ground to snatch
what they find.

### What the reading argues

That the state of nature is not only a device but a description, and that the
description was doing work: peoples said to have no government could be said
to have no property in the land, no sovereign to treat with, and no standing
to resist. Richard Tuck traced how the natural law tradition supplied those
arguments; David Armitage and Anthony Pagden put Hobbes inside the ideology of
empire rather than beside it; Barbara Arneil showed with Locke how much a
canonical text changes when read from the colonial archive.

The counter-argument is that Hobbes's example is a minor illustration, that his
constraint against extermination is real, and that his account of the law of
nations gives conquerors no more right than anyone else. Both sides are
reading the same page, which is what makes it worth reading closely.
