Leo Strauss put Hobbes at a hinge, and the whole reading follows from where he
put it. Before: classical and medieval political philosophy asking what the
best life is, and deriving duties from it. After: a politics that starts from
what people cannot help wanting and fearing, gives rights priority over
duties, and asks not what men should be but what can be built with them as
they are. Hobbes is the man who turns the corner.

### The moral basis

The claim that made the 1936 book famous is that the system does not rest on
the new science at all. Geometry supplies the method and the vocabulary, but
the foundation is a moral attitude: the fear of violent death, which Hobbes
treats as the one passion whose object every man agrees about, and which
supplies the fixed point that the summum bonum used to supply. Strauss's
Hobbes is therefore less modern than he claims — a moralist in mechanical
dress — and more radical, because his politics rests on the strongest passion
rather than on the highest good.

That is why [Chapter 11](/chapters/11-of-the-difference-of-manners/) matters so
much to this reading: the denial that there is any
[*finis ultimus* or *summum bonum*](/concepts/felicity/) is the demolition
charge, and everything classical goes down with it.

### Reading between the lines

The other Straussian habit is the one that attracts the most objection. On
this view a writer under threat of persecution — and Hobbes was in real danger
after 1666 — writes so that the careful reader finds the argument and the
careless one finds the orthodoxy. Applied to Parts III and IV, that yields a
Hobbes whose scriptural chapters are a demolition conducted politely, which
is roughly what his contemporaries suspected and what Devin Stauffer has
argued at length. Harvey Mansfield extended the account of what the founding
did to executive power.

### The objection

Quentin Skinner's, and it is not a detail. If the method allows a reader to
discount what a text says in favour of what it must have meant, the historical
evidence stops constraining the interpretation. The
[contextualist reply](/readings/contextualist/) is that Hobbes's arguments
were moves in identifiable quarrels, and that recovering the quarrel does more
work than any hidden doctrine.

Both sides agree on the importance of the question they disagree about: what
exactly happened to political philosophy in the seventeenth century, and
whether we are still living in the answer.
