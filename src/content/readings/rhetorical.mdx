[Chapter 5](/chapters/05-of-reason-and-science/) is unambiguous: reason is
reckoning, science is knowledge of consequences, and metaphors and senseless
speech are among the causes of absurdity. Eloquence gets a whole paragraph of
abuse. And yet nobody quotes anyone else's definition of the state of nature,
because nobody else wrote "solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short".

### Skinner's three phases

The account that organised the field is Quentin Skinner's, and it goes like
this. Hobbes was trained as a
humanist, in a curriculum that treated rhetoric and reason as partners, and his
early work shows it. Then, having discovered geometry, he repudiated the
partnership: *The Elements of Law* and *De Cive* argue that a true science of
politics needs no persuasion at all, since demonstration compels assent.

Then, having been ignored, he changed his mind again. *Leviathan* takes the
rhetorical arts back — the metaphors, the ridicule, the definitions that land
like verdicts, the images of the artificial man and the kingdom of fairies —
because a demonstration nobody reads persuades nobody. The book that attacks
eloquence is the one where he uses all of it.

### What the images are doing

David Johnston's argument is that the book is transformative rather than
merely instructive: its job is to produce readers capable of being subjects,
which is why so much of it is spent dismantling the beliefs that make people
ungovernable. Victoria Kahn puts the contract among the literary devices of
the century, beside the promises and oaths that the drama was worrying about.
Horst Bredekamp takes the frontispiece as a piece of the argument, not a
decoration: a body made of bodies, holding sword and crozier, with a verse from
Job across the top.

### Why it matters for reading him

Because it changes what counts as evidence. If the rhetoric is decoration, the
[chain of definitions](/definitions/) is the book and the rest is style. If the
rhetoric is doing work, then the images are arguments — the
[artificial man](/themes/artifice/), the
[diseases of the commonwealth](/themes/diseases-of-the-commonwealth/), the
[ghost of the Roman empire](/themes/christianity/) — and a reader who
paraphrases them away has lost part of the case.
