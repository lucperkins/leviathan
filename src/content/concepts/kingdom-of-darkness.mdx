Part IV takes its name from a phrase Hobbes finds in scripture rather than
invents. Chapter XLIV opens by noting that besides the sovereign powers already
discussed, divine and human, there is mention of another: the rulers of the
darkness of this world, the kingdom of Satan, the prince of the power of the
air.

What matters is what he does with it next. The kingdom of darkness is not a
place of punishment and not a spiritual realm. It is a condition of ignorance
maintained in the present world, and because ignorance is maintained by
someone, it can be investigated like any other political arrangement.

### Four causes

Chapter XLIV sets out the sources of the darkness, and the order is
deliberate. First and largest is the misinterpretation of scripture, above all
the reading of the kingdom of God as the present church, which converts a
promise about the resurrection into a claim of jurisdiction now. Then the
confusion of consecration with conjuration, so that a rite is taken to work on
the world rather than to signify. Then mistaken doctrines about eternal life
and everlasting death, which put purgatory and the invocation of saints on
foundations Hobbes thinks the text will not bear. Then the mixing in of vain
philosophy and fabulous traditions, which occupies Chapters XLV and XLVI.

### Where it comes from

The two middle chapters trace the inheritance. Chapter XLV argues that the
demonology of the schools is the religion of the gentiles carried into
Christianity, with the phantasms of the ancients renamed rather than removed.
Chapter XLVI turns on the universities: philosophy is defined there as
knowledge acquired by reasoning from the generation of a thing to its
properties, and the schools are convicted of failing that definition. Their
abstract essences and incorporeal substances are not so much false as
meaningless, names admitted to the argument without ever being given a sense.

This is what ties Part IV to Part I. The darkness is the failure of the
[chain of definitions](/definitions/): once a word enters without a definition,
anything can be proved with it, and someone will.

### Who profits

Chapter XLVII asks the question a lawyer would, and Hobbes takes his method
from a Roman judge:

> Cicero maketh honorable mention of one of the Cassii, a severe Judge amongst
> the Romans, for a custome he had, in Criminal causes, (when the testimony of
> the witnesses was not sufficient,) to ask the Accusers, Cui Bono.
>
> — Chapter XLVII

The answer he gives is the clergy, and chiefly the papacy: the doctrines that
constitute the darkness are, one by one, the doctrines that secure clerical
power and revenue against the civil sovereign. The chapter ends with the
comparison that gave his enemies most offence, between the papacy and the
kingdom of fairies — a realm of enchantments whose workshops, he notes, are
well enough known to be the universities.
