Chapter III arrives at it by way of the trains of imagination. A man who wants
to know how an action will turn out thinks of a like action past and the
events that followed it, supposing like events will follow like actions:

> As Prudence is a Praesumtion of the Future, contracted from the Experience
> of time Past; So there is a Praesumtion of things Past taken from other
> things (not future but) past also.

### Guessing, well or badly

Hobbes is careful to say that this is only conjecture, because the events of
things are never entirely alike; and that its quality depends on how much
experience a man has and how well he has attended to it. The prudent man is
the one who guesses best, not the one who knows. That is why prudence
increases with age and cannot be taught, whereas
[reason](/concepts/reason/) is attained by industry and can be — the
distinction is drawn sharply in Chapter V, where reason is said to be neither
born with us like sense and memory, nor got by experience only, as prudence
is.

### Not peculiar to us

Beasts have it. Anything that can remember can presume the future from the
past, and a creature that has learned where the danger lies is prudent in
exactly Hobbes's sense. The line that separates us from the other
[living creatures](/themes/animals/) is not prudence but
[speech](/concepts/speech/), and the whole of Part I is arranged so that this
is obvious by the time it matters.

### Why it will not do for politics

Because prudence generalises from cases, and cases can always be met with
other cases. A politics founded on the experience of statesmen is a politics
that can be argued with indefinitely — which is what Hobbes thought had
happened to England. Science, reckoning from definitions, is supposed to end
the argument. Whether it did is another matter, but the ambition explains the
shape of the book.
