The word arrives at the end of Chapter XVII almost in passing, once the
[commonwealth](/concepts/commonwealth/) has been defined:

> And he that carryeth this Person, as called SOVERAIGNE, and said to have
> Soveraigne Power; and every one besides, his SUBJECT.

It is a role, not a man. A monarch fills it, or an assembly does; what matters
is that some one [person](/concepts/person/) bears the person of the
multitude and that everybody else has authorised what that person does.

### Outside the covenant

The covenant is made by each subject with every other subject, not with the
sovereign. Hobbes builds it that way deliberately, and two things follow. The
sovereign cannot breach a covenant with his subjects, because he made none;
and the subjects cannot lawfully change the form of government, because they
would have to break faith with each other to do it. Chapter XVIII draws out
twelve rights from this — over doctrine, law, judicature, war and peace,
counsellors, rewards, honours — and insists they are indivisible: a power
divided is dissolved, and a kingdom divided in itself cannot stand.

### The office, and its limits

Chapter XXX is the other half, and the half that is usually forgotten. The
sovereign's power comes with a duty, and the duty is the safety of the
people — by which Hobbes does not mean bare survival:

> But by Safety here, is not meant a bare Preservation, but also all other
> Contentments of life, which every man by lawfull Industry, without danger,
> or hurt to the Common-wealth, shall acquire to himselfe.

He owes good laws, equal taxes, public charity for those who cannot work, the
prevention of idleness, and the teaching of the doctrines on which his own
authority rests. None of it is enforceable by subjects, which is the standing
objection to the whole arrangement; the sovereign answers to God alone. But
the obligation is real in Hobbes's terms, and so is its limit: obedience lasts
exactly as long as the power to protect.
