Chapter VII takes the word apart etymologically and the history is the
argument. When two or more men know one and the same fact, they are said to be
*conscious* of it to one another, which is as much as to know it together —
and it was always reckoned the worst of crimes for such men to speak against
what they knew. Then men used the word for their knowledge of their own secret
facts, and so of their own thoughts. Finally, Hobbes says, men gave their
opinions a reverence by calling them conscience, as if it were unlawful to
change or speak against them — and so pretend to know they are true, when they
know at most that they think so.

### Why he takes the trouble

Because the word was doing political work in 1651. If conscience is knowledge
shared, or knowledge of one's own act, it settles nothing about what may be
publicly done. If it is a name for strong opinion, then anyone who calls a
belief his conscience has claimed an authority no sovereign can inspect.

[Chapter 29](/chapters/29-of-those-things-that-weaken-or-tend-to-the-dissolution-of-a-common-wealth/)
lists the consequence among the diseases: the doctrine that whatsoever a man
does against his conscience is sin, which Hobbes says depends on the
presumption of making himself judge of good and evil. In a commonwealth the
law is the public conscience, by which a man has already undertaken to be
guided; and the private conscience, which is but private opinion, does not
release him from it.

### What is left to the inward man

Not nothing. Belief and unbelief never follow men's commands, and faith is not
a thing anybody can be ordered into; Hobbes is explicit that a subject who
inwardly believes otherwise is not obliged to say so, and that the sovereign
commands profession rather than persuasion. It is a narrow liberty, and
[Carl Schmitt](/further-reading/#schmitt-leviathan) thought it was the crack
through which the whole edifice would eventually be pulled apart.
