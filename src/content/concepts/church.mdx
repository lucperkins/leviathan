Chapter XXXIX runs through the senses the word carries in scripture — a house
of God, an assembly of citizens lawfully called, the whole body of the
faithful — and then settles on one:

> A company of men professing Christian Religion, united in the person of one
> Soveraign; at whose command they ought to assemble, and without whose
> authority they ought not to assemble.

### Every word already defined

That is the point of putting it here. *Religion* was fixed in Chapter VI,
[person](/concepts/person/) in XVI, [sovereign](/concepts/sovereign/) in XVII,
command and authority along the way. Nothing new is introduced, and a church
becomes a kind of commonwealth — or rather the same commonwealth, considered
as professing Christianity. Hobbes says so in as many words: a church and a
Christian commonwealth are all one thing.

### What follows

Two conclusions, and Part III is largely their elaboration. There is no
universal church on earth that all Christians are bound to obey, because there
is no power on earth to which all commonwealths are subject. And a church
that assembles without warrant from the civil sovereign is an unlawful
assembly, whatever it calls itself.

[Chapter 42](/chapters/42-of-power-ecclesiasticall/) then works out what is
left to pastors: teaching, which persuades and does not compel. Excommunication
binds nobody the sovereign has not obliged to be bound, and a king
excommunicated is simply a king. The long reply to
[Bellarmine](/touchstones/bellarmine/) at the end of that chapter is possible
only because this definition has already done the work.
