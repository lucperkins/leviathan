Few sentences in *Leviathan* did more damage to Hobbes's reputation than
his definition of good and evil, and few are more central to the book. It
comes in Chapter VI, immediately after the definitions of appetite and
aversion, as if it were the obvious next step:

> But whatsoever is the object of any mans Appetite or Desire; that is it,
> which he for his part calleth Good: And the object of his Hate, and
> Aversion, evill; And of his contempt, Vile, and Inconsiderable.

Good is not a property that things have and that desire responds to. It is
the name a person gives to what they already desire. The order of
explanation is reversed from the whole classical and Christian tradition,
in which one desires something *because* it is good; for Hobbes, one calls
it good *because* one desires it.

### Nothing simply so

Hobbes draws the consequence at once:

> For these words of Good, evill, and Contemptible, are ever used with
> relation to the person that useth them: There being nothing simply and
> absolutely so; nor any common Rule of Good and evill, to be taken from
> the nature of the objects themselves; but from the Person of the man
> (where there is no Common-wealth;) or, (in a Common-wealth,) from the
> Person that representeth it; or from an Arbitrator or Judge, whom men
> disagreeing shall by consent set up, and make his sentence the Rule
> thereof.

This one sentence contains the whole political consequence. Because good
and evil are relative to the person, and people differ, the words have no
settled meaning in the natural condition; each person is their own measure.
A shared meaning can only come from a person or body everyone has agreed to
let decide. That is one of the jobs the sovereign is for. The relativity of
good and evil is not, for Hobbes, a reason for tolerance; it is a reason
for authority.

### Varieties of good

Hobbes then sorts the term. The Latin *pulchrum* and *turpe* name what
promises good or evil by outward signs, roughly "fair" and "foul." Good can
be good *in the promise* (pulchrum), good *in the effect* (delightful), or
good *as a means* (profitable), with evil divided the same way. And in
deliberation, where we weigh the foreseen consequences of an action, the
balance of good over evil in the chain as far as we can see it is what
"Writers call Apparent or Seeming Good," since no one sees the chain to its
end.

### No greatest good

The rejection of any absolute good is completed in Chapter XI:

> For there is no such Finis Ultimus, (utmost ayme,) nor Summum Bonum,
> (greatest good,) as is spoken of in the Books of the old Morall
> Philosophers.
>
> — Chapter XI

There is no end at which desire could rest and call itself satisfied.
There is only the next object, and the one after it. The *Desire* entry
follows that thread; here the point is that without a highest good there is
no natural standard against which a sovereign's determination of good and
evil could be measured and found wanting.

### How morality returns

It would be easy to read all this as saying that morality is an illusion.
Hobbes does not. In Chapter XV, having derived the laws of nature, he
explains how objective moral terms re-enter a world where good and evil are
relative:

> And therefore so long as man is in the condition of meer Nature, (which is
> a condition of War,) as private Appetite is the measure of Good, and
> Evill: and consequently all men agree on this, that Peace is Good, and
> therefore also the way, or means of Peace, which (as I have shewed before)
> are Justice, Gratitude, Modesty, Equity, Mercy, & the rest of the Laws of
> Nature, are good; that is to say, Morall Vertues; and their contrarie
> Vices, Evill.
>
> — Chapter XV

Everyone desires peace, because everyone fears death. So peace is good for
everyone, in Hobbes's own sense of "good," and whatever is a means to peace
is good too. The moral virtues are simply the means to peace, and moral
philosophy is the science of them. Morality is not written into nature; it
is derived from the one thing all the relative appetites have in common.
