Chapter VI begins with a distinction between two kinds of motion in animals.
*Vital* motion is the kind that goes on without our attention: the blood,
the pulse, breathing, digestion. *Voluntary* motion is walking, speaking,
moving a limb, anything done "in such manner, as is first fancied in our
minds." Voluntary motion starts as imagination, and its first, invisible
stirrings inside the body Hobbes calls *endeavour*. Desire is endeavour with
a direction:

> This Endeavour, when it is toward something which causes it, is called
> APPETITE, or DESIRE; the later, being the generall name; and the other,
> oftentimes restrayned to signifie the Desire of Food, namely Hunger and
> Thirst.

> And when the Endeavour is fromward something, it is generally called
> AVERSION.

Everything in Hobbes's psychology is built from these two. Love and hate are
desire and aversion with the object present; hope is desire with an opinion
of attaining; fear is aversion with an opinion of hurt; courage, anger,
confidence, and the rest are further combinations. The passions are not a
separate faculty that struggles with reason; they are the directions in
which a body is already starting to move.

### Good is what is desired

The consequence that most shocked Hobbes's readers is that good and evil are
defined by desire, not the other way round:

> But whatsoever is the object of any mans Appetite or Desire; that is it,
> which he for his part calleth Good: And the object of his Hate, and
> Aversion, evill; And of his contempt, Vile, and Inconsiderable.

There is no good in itself, no standard written into the nature of things
that desire ought to track. "Good" is a word people attach to whatever they
happen to want, and since people want different things, the words carry no
shared meaning until a common authority fixes one. This is the seed of
Hobbes's politics inside his psychology: the sovereign is needed, among
other reasons, because without one "good" means as many things as there are
people.

### A machine that cannot stop wanting

The most striking claim comes at the end of Chapter VI and the start of
Chapter XI. The old moral philosophers had taught that human life aims at a
final end, a *summum bonum*, in which desire comes to rest. Hobbes denies
there is any such thing:

> For there is no such Finis Ultimus, (utmost ayme,) nor Summum Bonum,
> (greatest good,) as is spoken of in the Books of the old Morall
> Philosophers.
>
> — Chapter XI

Felicity, the closest thing to happiness in his vocabulary, is not a state
but a movement:

> Felicity is a continuall progresse of the desire, from one object to
> another; the attaining of the former, being still but the way to the
> later.
>
> — Chapter XI

And a life without desire is not a life at rest but rather no life at all:

> Nor can a man any more live, whose Desires are at an end, than he, whose
> Senses and Imaginations are at a stand.
>
> — Chapter XI

This is the human being as a machine for wanting: a body whose motion *is*
its desiring, which stops only when the body stops. From it follows the
"generall inclination of all mankind, a perpetuall and restlesse desire of
Power after power, that ceaseth onely in Death." Hobbes is careful about the
mechanism. It is not that people are greedy; it is that no one can secure
what they already have "without the acquisition of more." Desire is
unlimited because security is never complete, and that, as much as
competition or glory, is what makes the natural condition a war.

<Sidelight title="Deleuze and Guattari">

Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari open *Anti-Oedipus* (1972) with the image
of "desiring-machines": desire is not a lack in a subject that seeks to be
filled but rather a productive process, a machine that runs by connecting,
cutting, and connecting again, and that never arrives at a state of rest.
Strip away the three centuries and the vocabulary, and this is Hobbes's
human being. A body whose motion is desire; whose desire is not a want of
something missing but rather an endeavour that is always already under way;
which passes from object to object without any final object; and which
stops only when the machine stops. Hobbes says it in plainer words: a person
whose desires are at an end can no more live than one whose senses and
imaginations are at a stand. Humans, for Hobbes, are desiring-machines, and
*Leviathan* is an attempt to work out what kind of political order such
machines can build and live under.

The politics diverge completely. Deleuze and Guattari want desire freed from
every apparatus that captures and codes it, the state first of all; Hobbes
wants the machines assembled into one great artificial machine, the
common-wealth, precisely because their desiring is unlimited and cannot be
left to itself. But the disagreement is about what to do with desire, not
about what desire is. On that question, the seventeenth-century materialist
and the twentieth-century anti-Oedipals are closer to each other than either
is to the tradition of the *summum bonum* they both reject.

</Sidelight>
