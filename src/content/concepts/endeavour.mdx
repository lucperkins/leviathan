Chapter VI distinguishes two sorts of motion in animals. Vital motion — the
course of the blood, the pulse, breathing — needs no imagination and never
stops. Voluntary motion — going, speaking, moving a limb — is always first
imagined, and between the imagining and the visible act there is something
too small to see:

> These small beginnings of Motion, within the body of Man, before they appear
> in walking, speaking, striking, and other visible actions, are commonly
> called ENDEAVOUR.

### Why the word matters

It is the hinge of the system. Part I has explained
[sense](/concepts/sense/) and [imagination](/concepts/imagination/) as motions
caused by motions; if the passions are also motions, then the whole of human
conduct is continuous with physics and can be reasoned about in the same way.
Endeavour is the term that carries the argument across. An endeavour toward
the thing that causes it is [appetite](/concepts/desire/); away from it,
aversion. Everything afterwards — love, hope, fear, courage, ambition — is
one of these, named by what it is toward and in what circumstances.

### No summum bonum

Because endeavour is motion, it does not stop. There is no utmost aim or
greatest good, as the old moral philosophers spoke of, because
[felicity](/concepts/felicity/) is a continual progress of desire from one
object to another, the attaining of the former being still but the way to the
latter. A creature made of endeavours cannot arrive anywhere and rest, and a
politics for such creatures has to be built on what they fear rather than on
what they are for.

### An unusual afterlife

The word had a technical life in the mechanics of the period, and Hobbes uses
it in *De Corpore* for the beginning of motion in a point, which is close to
what the calculus would later handle with limits. In *Leviathan* he asks it to
do the work of a psychology, and the whole account of the passions rests on
that single borrowed term.
