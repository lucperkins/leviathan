The *common-wealth* is the thing the whole book is about: the artificial
person Hobbes calls, in his title, the Leviathan. Parts I and II of the book
are a single argument that runs from sense and imagination, through the
passions and the state of nature, to the conclusion that people can only
live securely by creating one.

### Why it is needed

In the natural condition, without a common power to hold everyone in awe,
each person is judge of their own safety and every person is a potential
threat to every other. Agreements alone will not fix this, because "Covenants,
without the Sword, are but Words." What is required is a power strong enough
that keeping one's word is safer than breaking it.

### How it is made

Hobbes describes the founding act in Chapter XVII:

> The only way to erect such a Common Power, as may be able to defend them
> from the invasion of Forraigners, and the injuries of one another … is, to
> conferre all their power and strength upon one Man, or upon one Assembly of
> men, that may reduce all their Wills, by plurality of voices, unto one Will.
>
> — Chapter XVII

Every member of the multitude covenants with every other to *authorise* the
acts of that one person or assembly as their own. The sovereign is therefore
not a party to the covenant; the covenant is between the subjects, and the
sovereign is its product. This is why, for Hobbes, the sovereign cannot
breach it.

### The definition

> And in him consisteth the Essence of the Common-wealth; which (to define
> it,) is "One Person, of whose Acts a great Multitude, by mutuall Covenants
> one with another, have made themselves every one the Author, to the end he
> may use the strength and means of them all, as he shall think expedient,
> for their Peace and Common Defence."
>
> — Chapter XVII

The person who bears this role is the **Soveraign**; everyone else is a
**Subject**. Hobbes calls the result "that Mortall God, to which wee owe
under the Immortall God, our peace and defence."

### Two routes

A common-wealth by *institution* is created when a multitude voluntarily
agrees to submit to a sovereign. A common-wealth by *acquisition* is created
when people submit to a conqueror out of fear of death. Hobbes insists the
rights of the sovereign are the same in both cases, a point his contemporaries
found provocative, since it treated a usurper and a lawful king alike.
