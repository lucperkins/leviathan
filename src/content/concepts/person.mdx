Chapter XVI is the shortest chapter in Part I and the one the whole of Part
II depends on. Before Hobbes can say what a common-wealth is, he has to
explain how many people can act as one, and the word he uses for that is
*person*:

> A PERSON, is he "whose words or actions are considered, either as his
> own, or as representing the words or actions of an other man, or of any
> other thing to whom they are attributed, whether Truly or by Fiction.

> When they are considered as his owne, then is he called a Naturall
> Person: And when they are considered as representing the words and
> actions of an other, then is he a Feigned or Artificiall person.

A person, then, is not a kind of being but rather a way of *considering*
words and actions: as belonging to the one who produces them, or as
standing for someone else's. The same human being is a natural person when
speaking for themselves and an artificial person when speaking for a
client, a ward, or a company.

### The mask

Hobbes grounds the term in its history:

> The word Person is latine: instead whereof the Greeks have Prosopon,
> which signifies the Face, as Persona in latine signifies the Disguise, or
> Outward Appearance of a man, counterfeited on the Stage; and somtimes more
> particularly that part of it, which disguiseth the face, as a Mask or
> Visard

A person is originally a mask, and to personate is to act a part. Hobbes
takes the theatrical sense seriously: to represent someone is to wear their
face, and the words spoken through the mask are attributed to the character,
not to the actor.

### Actor, author, authority

The next distinction carries the political weight:

> Of Persons Artificiall, some have their words and actions Owned by those
> whom they represent. And then the Person is the Actor; and he that owneth
> his words and actions, is the AUTHOR: In which case the Actor acteth by
> Authority. For that which in speaking of goods and possessions, is called
> an Owner, and in latine Dominus, in Greeke Kurios; speaking of Actions, is
> called Author.

An *actor* performs the words and actions; the *author* owns them; the
actor's right to perform them is *authority*. The consequence Hobbes wants
is that a covenant made by an actor with authority binds the author, exactly
as if the author had made it in person. This is the mechanism by which
subjects will later be bound by whatever their sovereign does.

### What can be personated

Almost anything, Hobbes says: "There are few things, that are uncapable of
being represented by Fiction." Inanimate things such as a church, a
hospital, or a bridge can be personated by a rector, master, or overseer.
Children and madmen can be personated by guardians. The false gods of the
heathen were personated by their priests. And "The true God may be
Personated," as he was by Moses, by Christ, and by the apostles. That last
claim leads Hobbes, in Part III, into an account of the Trinity that his
contemporaries found alarming.

### One person out of many

The chapter's climax is the sentence that makes the common-wealth possible:

> A Multitude of men, are made One Person, when they are by one man, or one
> Person, Represented; so that it be done with the consent of every one of
> that Multitude in particular.

> For it is the Unity of the Representer, not the Unity of the Represented,
> that maketh the Person One.

A multitude is not a person and cannot act; it is many people, with many
wills. It becomes one person only by being represented by one, and it is the
representer who supplies the unity. Every member of the multitude authorises
the representer individually, and so each is the author of everything the
representer does. Chapter XVII simply applies this: the common-wealth is "the
Multitude so united in one Person," and the sovereign is the one who bears
that person.
