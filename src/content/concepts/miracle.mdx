> A MIRACLE, is a work of God, (besides his operation by the way of Nature,
> ordained in the Creation,) done for the making manifest to his elect, the
> mission of an extraordinary Minister for their salvation.

Every clause is load-bearing. It must be God's work and not nature's; it must
be *besides* the course he ordained, so ordinary providence does not count;
and it has a purpose, which is to accredit a messenger.

### Admiration, and ignorance

Because the point is to make something manifest, a miracle is relative to the
observer. What is strange to one man is not strange to another who knows the
cause: an eclipse astonishes those who have never seen one and nobody who can
predict it. Hobbes takes this a long way. Since men are apt to take for
miracles what is only unusual, and since juggling and imposture can produce
the unusual on demand, the credit of a wonder depends on the credit of those
who report it — which is why the definition ends up handing the question to
the sovereign, who is judge of what miracles are to be publicly acknowledged.

### And they have ceased

Miracles were for the founding of the faith and are not needed to maintain
it. Since the time of the apostles there have been no such works, and the
claims made for later ones are either mistakes about causes or frauds
maintained by people with an interest in them. That conclusion belongs with
the argument of Part IV: a standing supply of wonders is a standing supply of
authority outside the commonwealth, and Hobbes is closing off the supply.

For a reader who wants to see what he thought that supply was worth, the
[kingdom of darkness](/concepts/kingdom-of-darkness/) is the account of what
grew in it.
