Chapter XXXIV is the hinge of Part III, and it opens by saying why: the
foundation of all true reasoning is the constant signification of words, and
in what follows that signification depends neither on the writer's will nor
on common use, but on what the scripture actually means by them.

### The word

Taken properly, *spirit* means a subtle, fluid, invisible body — wind, breath,
the aerial substances of the old physiology. Taken metaphorically it means a
disposition or inclination of the mind: a spirit of contradiction, an unclean
spirit, a froward spirit. Neither of these is what the schools want. If
corporeal is taken in the vulgar sense, for substances perceptible by our
senses, then an incorporeal substance is real enough — a thin substance,
invisible, but having the same dimensions as grosser bodies. If it is taken
strictly, "incorporeal substance" joins two words that destroy each other.

### The stakes

Almost everything Hobbes wants from Part III runs through this. If there are
no incorporeal substances, then separated souls do not walk, purgatory has
nowhere to be, exorcism is a ceremony without an object, and the whole traffic
in ghosts and possessions that the churches had inherited from the heathen is
a misreading of ordinary words. And if the soul is not naturally immortal,
[eternal life](/concepts/kingdom-of-darkness/) is a gift of grace at the
resurrection rather than a property of human nature.

### Angels, and inspiration

The same method handles the rest of the chapter's title. Angels in scripture
are messengers — sometimes real substances, sometimes images raised
supernaturally in the fancy — and inspiration is a metaphor drawn from
breathing rather than a description of God pouring something into a man.
Hobbes grants that God may work by whatever means he pleases; what he denies
is that anyone may build a doctrine, or a claim to obedience, on a word whose
meaning has never been fixed.

<Sidelight title="Ryle and the category mistake">

Gilbert Ryle opens *The Concept of Mind* (1949) by giving a name to a kind of
error: the visitor shown the colleges, the library and the playing fields who
then asks where the University is has put a thing in the wrong logical
category. The book spends four hundred pages arguing that the mind, conceived
as a substance running the body from inside it, is that error — the ghost in
the machine.

Hobbes makes the same move three centuries earlier and in six words. An
incorporeal substance is not a false description of something; it is two words
that destroy each other, like a round quadrangle, and a sentence built out of
it is not a claim but a noise. The two arguments differ in what they are aimed
at — Ryle at Descartes, Hobbes at the schoolmen — and Ryle was not reading
Hobbes when he made it. The shapes are the same anyway: both refuse the
question of *where* the immaterial thing is on the ground that the question
cannot be asked, and both are accused of having explained the mind away rather
than explained it.

What Hobbes pays for the position, Ryle does not. If nothing is incorporeal,
and God is not nothing, then God is a body — which Hobbes concedes in the
appendix to the Latin *Leviathan* and which his answerers, with some justice,
called atheism by another route. Ryle had the luxury of stopping at the
mind.

</Sidelight>
