> A LAW OF NATURE, (Lex Naturalis,) is a Precept, or generall Rule, found out
> by Reason, by which a man is forbidden to do, that, which is destructive of
> his life, or taketh away the means of preserving the same; and to omit,
> that, by which he thinketh it may be best preserved.

Two words are doing the work. *Found out by reason*: these are not commands
issued by anyone, they are conclusions anybody can reach. And *destructive of
his life*: the whole system is derived from self-preservation, not from a
notion of the good.

### Theorems, not commands

Hobbes says at the end of Chapter XV that these dictates of reason are
improperly called laws at all. A law is the word of him that by right has
command over others; these are conclusions about what conduces to
preservation. They become laws in the strict sense only when a sovereign
commands them, or when they are considered as delivered in the word of God.
That qualification is easy to read past and it decides how the whole book
hangs together.

### In foro interno

The most consequential passage in the chapter says they always bind in *foro
interno* — that is, to a desire that they take place — but not always in
*foro externo*, not to putting them in act. To perform first, where nobody
else will, is to make oneself a prey to others, and that is contrary to the
laws of nature themselves, which aim at preservation. So the laws are always
binding on the will and only sometimes on the hand, and what closes the gap
is a common power. The same limitation applies to sovereigns among
themselves, which is all Hobbes has to say about the
[law of nations](/themes/law/).

### The nineteen

They begin with the two of Chapter XIV — seek peace, and lay down the
[right of nature](/concepts/right-of-nature/) as far as others will — and
continue through Chapter XV: perform your covenants, which is
[justice](/concepts/justice/); gratitude; accommodation; pardon on
repentance; punishment aimed at future good only; against contumely, pride
and arrogance; equity; and the rest. Hobbes ends by compressing them into one
rule for anyone who cannot follow the reasoning: do not that to another,
which thou wouldest not have done to thy selfe.
