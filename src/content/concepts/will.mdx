Chapter VI describes deliberation first: appetites and aversions, hopes and
fears about one and the same thing arising alternately, until one of them
carries the action or the omission. Then:

> In Deliberation, the last Appetite, or Aversion, immediately adhaering to
> the action, or to the omission thereof, is that wee call the WILL; the Act,
> (not the faculty,) of Willing.

### Why "not the faculty"

The parenthesis is the whole argument. On the schoolmen's account the will is
a power of the soul that chooses among the objects reason presents, and is
itself undetermined — which is what makes the choice free. Hobbes denies there
is any such power. What there is, is a sequence of appetites, and the one that
happens to be last before the act is what we name the will. Nothing does the
willing; a willing occurs.

Two things follow. Beasts deliberate and therefore have wills, since they too
have alternating appetites. And a will is caused, like any other
[endeavour](/concepts/endeavour/), which is where the trouble starts.

### Voluntary, and free

If the will is the last appetite, a voluntary act is one proceeding from it —
and fear can supply the appetite as well as anything else. So a man who obeys
from terror acts voluntarily, and a promise extracted by fear can bind. This
is why Hobbes can say that fear and [liberty](/concepts/liberty/) are
consistent, and why he can allow that a commonwealth acquired by conquest
rests on a covenant as genuine as one made among equals.

The other consequence is determinism, and Hobbes accepts it without flinching:
every will is caused, the causes run back in a chain, and liberty consists in
the absence of impediments rather than in the absence of causes. The
[argument with Bramhall](/works/of-liberty-and-necessity/) about what that
leaves of praise, blame, sin and punishment ran from 1645 until neither man
would write another word.
