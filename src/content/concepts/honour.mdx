Chapter X is the coldest page in the book, and one of the most modern:

> The Value, or WORTH of a man, is as of all other things, his Price; that is
> to say, so much as would be given for the use of his Power: and therefore is
> not absolute; but a thing dependant on the need and judgement of another.

No amount of virtue makes a man worth anything if nobody needs him. An able
commander is of great price in war and of little in peace, and a learned judge
of little price except where there are courts. Worth is a market rate, and the
seller does not set it.

### Honour is an opinion of power

What follows is a long list, and its method is the argument. To pray to
another for aid is to honour him, because it signifies an opinion that he has
power to help; to obey is to honour, since no one obeys those he thinks have
no power to help or hurt; to give gifts, to yield the way, to speak
considerately, to imitate — all of these are honour, and their opposites
dishonour, for the same reason in each case. Titles, coats of arms and offices
are honour because they are signs of the sovereign's valuation, which is what
*dignity* means.

Nothing here is about desert. Honour is a report of power, and the honourable
is simply whatever argues power.

### Why it matters later

Because this is what men fight over. Chapter XIII gives glory as the third
cause of quarrel, and glory is this: men fight for trifles, a word, a smile, a
different opinion, and any other sign of undervalue. In
[Chapter 17](/chapters/17-of-the-causes-generation-and-definition-of-a-common-wealth/)
the same fact separates us from the bees — they are without the competition
for honour and dignity that sets men against each other. A creature that reads
every gesture as a valuation of itself cannot be left to arrange its own
society.
