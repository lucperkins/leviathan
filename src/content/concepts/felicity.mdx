> Continual Successe in obtaining those things which a man from time to time
> desireth, that is to say, continual prospering, is that men call FELICITY; I
> mean the Felicity of this life.

The definition is deliberately thin, and the sentence after it is the point:
there is no such thing as perpetual tranquillity of mind while we live here,
because life itself is motion and can never be without desire, or without
fear, any more than without sense.

### The denial of a highest good

Chapter XI states the consequence flatly: there is no *Finis Ultimus*, utmost
aim, nor *Summum Bonum*, greatest good, as is spoken of in the books of the
old moral philosophers. Felicity is a continual progress of desire from one
object to another, the attaining of the former being still but the way to the
latter. A creature whose life is [endeavour](/concepts/endeavour/) cannot
arrive.

That single move rules out the entire ancient tradition. If there is no
highest good, politics cannot be the art of bringing men to it, and no
argument about the best life can settle what a state is for. What is left is
the thing everyone agrees about in the other direction — violent death — and
a state built on avoiding it.

### Power after power

The famous inclination of Chapter XI follows immediately, and follows from
this: a perpetual and restless desire of power after power, that ceaseth only
in death. Hobbes is explicit that this is not because men always hope for more
intense delight, or cannot be content with moderate power, but because they
cannot assure the power and means to live well which they have at present
without the acquisition of more. Restlessness is a consequence of insecurity,
not of greed — which makes the whole system a good deal less cynical than its
reputation, and a good deal harder to escape.

### A sidelight: the hedonic treadmill

In 1971 Philip Brickman and Donald Campbell gave the thing a name. Pleasure
and pain, they argued, are registered as departures from a level a person has
adapted to, so a gain raises the level and the gain stops being felt; the
chase can be run indefinitely without arriving anywhere. Seven years later
Brickman and two colleagues went looking for the effect in the two most
extreme groups they could find, lottery winners and people paralysed in
accidents, and reported that both had drifted back toward where they started
much further than anyone expected.

Hobbes has the conclusion in Chapter XI, and reaches it by deduction from
motion rather than by asking anybody anything. Felicity is a continual
progress of the desire from one object to another; the attaining of the former
is still but the way to the latter; there is no *finis ultimus* and no *summum
bonum*, and a mind at repose is a mind that has stopped. It is the same shape
of claim, and the empirical work is closer to a confirmation than a
coincidence.

Where they part is on what it is evidence for. The psychologists take
adaptation as a fact about pleasure, and the practical moral drawn from it is
usually personal — chase the wrong things and you will not get anywhere.
Hobbes takes restlessness as a fact about power and insecurity, and the moral
he draws is political: creatures built this way cannot be left to arrange
their own peace. The treadmill, for him, is not a mistake people make. It is
the reason they need a sovereign.
