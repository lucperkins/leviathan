Chapter XIV opens with a definition and a warning that it is not the one the
reader expects:

> The RIGHT OF NATURE, which Writers commonly call Jus Naturale, is the
> Liberty each man hath, to use his own power, as he will himselfe, for the
> preservation of his own Nature; that is to say, of his own Life; and
> consequently, of doing any thing, which in his own Judgement, and Reason,
> hee shall conceive to be the aptest means thereunto.

### A liberty, not a claim

A right here is a *liberty*: the absence of an obligation not to. It puts
nobody under a duty. My right of nature does not oblige you to leave me
alone, because you have the same right against me, and both of us are judges
of what our preservation requires. Hobbes is careful about this and later
writers were not, which is why "right" in his sense and "rights" in the modern
sense are almost opposites: his is a permission that everyone has and nobody
can enforce.

### Why it extends to everything

Because each man is the judge of the means. If I sincerely judge that my
safety requires your goods, or your life, then the right of nature covers it —
and so, Hobbes says, in this condition every man has a right to every thing,
even to one another's body. A right that everyone holds over everything is
worth nothing to anybody, which is the argument's turn: the right is not
denied, it is shown to be useless, and the way out is to lay part of it down
by [contract](/concepts/contract/).

### Against the law of nature

Right and [law](/concepts/law-of-nature/) are opposites and Hobbes says so
flatly: right consists in liberty to do or forbear, law determines and binds
to one of them, so that law and right differ as much as obligation and
liberty. Writers who confound them, he adds, have confounded the whole
subject. The second law of nature then tells a man to lay this right down as
far as others will — not because the right was wrong, but because keeping it
is fatal.
