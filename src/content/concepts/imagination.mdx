Hobbes's account of *imagination* follows directly from his account of sense
and from a principle borrowed from the new physics: a body in motion stays in
motion until something stops it. When an object stops pressing on the senses,
the motion it started in us does not vanish at once; it fades, the way a wave
keeps rolling after the wind drops.

> Imagination therefore is nothing but Decaying Sense; and is found in men,
> and many other living Creatures, as well sleeping, as waking.

### One thing, many names

Hobbes is fond of showing that words the schools treat as distinct faculties
name the same motion looked at from different angles. Imagination and memory
are his first example:

> This Decaying Sense, when wee would express the thing it self, (I mean
> Fancy it selfe,) wee call Imagination, as I said before; But when we would
> express the Decay, and signifie that the Sense is fading, old, and past, it
> is called Memory. So that Imagination and Memory, are but one thing, which
> for divers considerations hath divers names.

From here a short chain of definitions follows. "Much memory, or memory of
many things, is called Experience." Simple imagination reproduces something
once sensed, such as a man or a horse; compound imagination combines them,
as when "we conceive in our mind a Centaure." Fiction, in Hobbes's system, is
not a separate power but rather recombination.

### Dreams and apparitions

"The imaginations of them that sleep, are those we call Dreams." Because the
senses are numbed in sleep, dreams are not produced by outside objects but rather by
the agitation of the inner parts of the body. This is why dreams are more
vivid than waking imagination: no fresh sensation is present to dim them.
Hobbes uses the point to explain ghosts, visions, and much of pagan religion
as the misreading of dreams by people who could not tell them from waking
sight, a theme he returns to at length in the later parts of the book.

### Understanding

The chapter closes by defining understanding as a kind of imagination too:

> The Imagination that is raysed in man (or any other creature indued with
> the faculty of imagining) by words, or other voluntary signes, is that we
> generally call Understanding; and is common to Man and Beast.

What is peculiar to humans is not understanding as such, but rather understanding
*speech*: the ability to have images raised by the names and sequences of
names that make up language. That is the bridge to Chapter III, on how
imaginations follow one another in a train, and to Chapter IV, on speech.
