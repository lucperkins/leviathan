Chapter XXI opens with a definition designed to make a great deal of
seventeenth century political rhetoric unusable:

> Liberty, or FREEDOME, signifieth (properly) the absence of Opposition; (by
> Opposition, I mean externall Impediments of motion;) and may be applyed no
> lesse to Irrational, and Inanimate creatures, than to Rationall.

Water in a channel is free when the banks do not stop it. A man is free in
exactly the same sense.

### Fear and liberty are consistent

If liberty is the absence of external impediments, then a man who throws his
goods into the sea to save his ship does it freely: he could have refused,
and he willed to throw them. So do subjects who obey out of fear of the
sovereign. Being frightened into a choice is still choosing, because fear
works through the will rather than around it. This is where Hobbes parts
company with almost everyone: he refuses the idea that a coerced act is
therefore not the agent's own.

### Liberty and necessity are consistent too

The same argument, run further. Every act of will has a cause, and that cause
another, back to the first cause, which is God; so every voluntary act
proceeds from necessity and is nevertheless free, because freedom is about
impediments and not about causes. Hobbes and
[Bishop Bramhall](/hobbes/faith/) spent twenty years on this, and the
[paper that started it](/works/of-liberty-and-necessity/) is the clearest
thing he wrote on the subject.

### The liberty of subjects

What is left for a subject, then? Not a share in government, and not immunity
from the sovereign's power. The liberty of subjects lies in the things the
sovereign has not regulated — the silence of the law — where each man may do
as his own reason suggests: buy and sell, choose a trade, a diet, a home, and
educate his children. And one liberty cannot be given away at all, because no
covenant can oblige a man not to defend his own life. Quentin Skinner's
[*Hobbes and Republican Liberty*](/after-hobbes/) is an account of what this
definition was built to destroy: the rival idea that a man dependent on
another's will is unfree even if that will is never exercised.
