### Who he was

Plato was born into an aristocratic Athenian family during the Peloponnesian
War, became a follower of Socrates, and after Socrates' execution in 399 BC
turned from any prospect of an Athenian political career to philosophy. In
the 380s he founded a school in a grove outside the city walls sacred to the
hero Academus, from which the word "academy" descends. His surviving works are
dialogues, most of them with Socrates as the main speaker, covering
knowledge, the soul, love, language, law, and above all the question of how a
city should be ruled. The *Republic* answers that question with the
philosopher-king: cities will have no rest from evils until philosophers rule
or rulers philosophise.

Through late antiquity Plato's thought was reworked into Neoplatonism, and
by that route, and through Augustine, it shaped Christian theology long
before Aristotle's works were recovered. In Hobbes's own century Plato was
enjoying a revival among the Cambridge Platonists, who used him against
exactly the kind of materialism Hobbes stood for.

### Hobbes's target

Hobbes names Plato only a handful of times in *Leviathan*, far less often
than Aristotle, and usually in the same breath: "Plato, Aristotle, Cicero,
and divers others" who wrote about what law *is* without being lawyers. The
scholastic philosophy Hobbes attacks in Part IV is Aristotelian in its
vocabulary, so Aristotle takes most of the fire. But two of the ideas Hobbes
most wants to destroy are Platonic in origin.

The first is the doctrine of *separated essences*: the idea that there exist
immaterial things, forms or universals, apart from the bodies that have
them. For Hobbes, who holds that everything real is body, this is the root
error of all the "vain philosophy" of the schools, and it is Plato's before
it is anyone's. The second is the immaterial soul. Hobbes's account of the
afterlife in Part III is written against the Greek notion of a naturally
immortal soul, which he regards as an import from "the Daemonology of the
Heathen" rather than anything found in scripture.

### The one admiring line

Hobbes does grant Plato a real distinction. In the chapter on darkness from
vain philosophy he writes that "Plato that was the best Philosopher of the
Greeks, forbad entrance into his Schoole, to all that were not already in
some measure Geometricians." Geometry was, for Hobbes, the only science
humanity had so far got right, and the model for how he meant to do
political philosophy: from definitions, step by step, to conclusions that
could not be denied. That Plato demanded it of his students counted heavily
in his favour.

### The Common-wealth of Plato

The most revealing passage comes at the end of Part II. Having set out the
whole doctrine of sovereignty, Hobbes pauses to wonder whether anyone will
ever act on it:

> I am at the point of believing this my labour, as uselesse, and the
> Common-wealth of Plato; For he also is of opinion that it is impossible
> for the disorders of State, and change of Governments by Civill Warre, ever
> to be taken away, till Soveraigns be Philosophers.
>
> — Chapter XXXI

Then he recovers. Plato, he says, demanded too much: sovereigns need not be
mathematicians, as Plato required, but only understand "the Science of
Naturall Justice," and since "neither Plato, nor any other Philosopher
hitherto, hath put into order, and sufficiently, or probably proved all the
Theoremes of Morall doctrine," Hobbes hopes his own short and clear book
might do what the *Republic* could not: fall into the hands of a sovereign
who will read it without an interpreter and put it into practice.

The comparison is exact and a little vain. Hobbes casts himself as Plato's
successor in the one project he thinks worth attempting, while claiming to
have succeeded where Plato failed, by replacing an ideal city built on
forms with a real one built on fear.
