[Spinoza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baruch_Spinoza) is the one kindred spirit who worked after
*Leviathan* rather than before it, and the one who went furthest. The
[*Tractatus Theologico-Politicus*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tractatus_Theologico-Politicus)
(*Theological-Political Treatise*) appeared anonymously in 1670 and was denounced across Europe in the terms Hobbes had
been denounced in twenty years earlier.

### Where they agree

On scripture, remarkably closely. Both read the Bible as a historical document
with human authors, ask who actually wrote the Pentateuch and when — the
question of
[Chapter 33](/chapters/33-of-the-number-antiquity-scope-authority-and-interpreters-of-the-books-of-holy-scripture/)
— and conclude that prophecy is accommodated to the imagination of the
prophet and teaches obedience rather than truth. Both hold that the outward
practice of religion belongs to the sovereign and that a church with
independent authority is a standing danger.

On politics, they share the premise that right extends as far as power does,
and that a state is to be explained by what men are rather than what they
ought to be.

### Where they part

Spinoza said it himself, in a letter of 1674: the difference between them is
that he always preserves natural right entire. In his commonwealth the subject
transfers the exercise of his power but never his right, which remains as a
permanent measure of what the sovereign can actually do; a sovereign who
provokes his subjects beyond bearing loses his right along with his power. For
Hobbes the covenant gives up the right of governing oneself, and only the
right of self-defence is retained.

The consequence is a different politics. Spinoza argues that democracy is the
most natural form and that freedom of philosophising can be granted without
danger to peace; Hobbes thinks both propositions are how commonwealths die.

### Did Hobbes read him?

He could have: the *Tractatus* was in circulation in his last decade, and a
story reported at second hand has him saying its author had cut through him by
a bar's length — that he had not dared write so boldly. Whether the remark is
genuine or not, the judgment in it is sound.
