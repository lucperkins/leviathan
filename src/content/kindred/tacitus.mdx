For fifty years either side of 1600, [Tacitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacitus)
was the political textbook of educated Europe. Where an earlier generation had
read Cicero and learned to praise the republic, this one read the *Annals* and
learned how a monarchy is actually held: by managing appearances, by
understanding what men fear and want, and by attending to what is done rather
than what is professed. The habit had a name, Tacitism, and a politics, reason
of state.

### Where they agree

On the method of description. Tacitus explains an action by the interest it
served and the fear it answered, never by the virtue it displayed, and he
treats public professions of principle as moves. Hobbes does the same to
doctrines: [Chapter 29](/chapters/29-of-those-things-that-weaken-or-tend-to-the-dissolution-of-a-common-wealth/)
lists the opinions that dissolved the commonwealth and asks in each case who
benefits, which is the question Tacitus always asks.

They agree, too, that the arts of rule are arts. Neither thinks a state runs
on the goodness of the ruler, and neither is embarrassed to say so.

### Where they part

Tacitus is a moralist, and a bitter one. He describes tyranny in order to
condemn it, and his sympathies are with the senatorial dead. Hobbes has no
category of tyranny at all — he says the name means monarchy disliked — and
regards the whole Roman vocabulary of liberty as the thing that ruins states.
The tradition Tacitus belongs to taught Englishmen to admire republics, and
Hobbes blamed the [reading of Greek and Latin authors](/themes/the-english-civil-war/)
for the civil war.

### Did Hobbes read him?

He wrote about him. A *Discourse upon the Beginning of Tacitus*, published
anonymously in 1620, has been attributed to Hobbes on stylistic evidence and is
now generally accepted as his — which would make his earliest political writing
a line-by-line commentary on the first chapter of the *Annals*, thirty years
before *Leviathan* and in a style he later disowned.
