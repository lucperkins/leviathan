[Grotius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugo_Grotius) published
[*De Jure Belli ac Pacis*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_jure_belli_ac_pacis) in 1625, in exile,
having escaped prison in a book chest. It is the founding text of modern
natural law, and it was on the desk of everyone who wrote about politics for
the next century, Hobbes included.

### Where they agree

That the rules can be derived. Grotius's famous provocation is that natural
law would hold its force even if we were to grant what cannot be granted
without wickedness, that there is no God: the obligations follow from what
human beings are. That move — grounding law in a nature anyone can inspect,
rather than in revelation or authority — is what makes both of them modern,
and it is the move the theologians of both centuries objected to.

They agree, too, that self-preservation comes first, that promises bind, and
that the law of nations is the law of nature applied to states, which is the
sentence Hobbes disposes of the whole subject in.

### Where they part

On sociability. Grotius grounds the whole structure in an *appetitus
societatis*, a natural desire for company and ordered society that men have
before any agreement. Hobbes denies it flatly: men seek company for honour or
profit, and left to themselves are in a condition of war. Take sociability
away and the law of nature can no longer bind in the ordinary sense, which is
why Hobbes must make it bind
[in foro interno](/chapters/15-of-other-lawes-of-nature/) only, and why he
needs a sovereign where Grotius does not.

The consequence runs on into the
[law of nations](/themes/law/): for Grotius its rules genuinely constrain
sovereigns; for Hobbes they oblige to a desire and no further.

### Did Hobbes read him?

Certainly. *De Jure Belli ac Pacis* was inescapable, and the closest readers
of both have always suspected that Hobbes wrote his laws of nature with a copy
open. He is on the list of
[names a reader expects and does not find](/thinkers/#surprising-omissions).
