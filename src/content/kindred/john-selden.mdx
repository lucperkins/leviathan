[Selden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Selden) was a lawyer, an orientalist, a member of the Long
Parliament, and by common consent the best-read man in England. Hobbes cites
his *Titles of Honor* in
[Chapter 10](/chapters/10-of-power-worth-dignity-honour-and-worthiness/) —
one of very few contemporaries to be named in the book at all — and Aubrey
says the two were friends in Hobbes's later years.

### Where they agree

That an obligation needs a superior. Selden's *De Jure Naturali et Gentium
juxta Disciplinam Ebraeorum* (*On the Law of Nature and of Nations
According to the Discipline of the Hebrews*, 1640) rejects the idea that natural law binds
because reason perceives its rightness: a law obliges because a superior with
power to punish has commanded it, and the natural law binding all mankind is
what God laid on the sons of Noah. Strip out the Hebrew scholarship and the
structure is Hobbes's — law is command, not discovered reason, and where there
is no power to enforce there is no proper law.

They agree, too, about the church. Selden argued the Erastian case at the
Westminster Assembly against the Presbyterian divines, denying that a
consistory could excommunicate on its own authority. That is
[Chapter 42](/chapters/42-of-power-ecclesiasticall/), fought out in London
while Hobbes was in Paris.

### Where they part

In method, entirely. Selden gets his answers from the archive: rabbinic
sources, records, charters, the accumulated evidence of what has actually been
held. Hobbes wanted a politics that could be demonstrated from definitions and
distrusted precedent as such — the difference that makes his
[Dialogue of the common laws](/works/dialogue-of-the-common-laws/) an attack
on exactly the kind of learning Selden embodied.

### Did Hobbes read him?

Yes, and said so. Selden is the one kindred spirit in this list who appears in
*Leviathan* under his own name, and among the
[thinkers named](/thinkers/#contemporaries-and-near-contemporaries) he is one
of only a handful of moderns.
