[Machiavelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niccol%C3%B2_Machiavelli) had been dead a century and his
name was already an English adjective for the thing Hobbes would shortly be
accused of. The two are permanently filed together, usually by people who
dislike both.

### Where they agree

On the starting point. [*The Prince*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Prince) says men are ungrateful,
fickle, liars and evaders of danger, and that a ruler who assumes otherwise is
ruined; that it is safer to be feared than loved, because love is held by a
chain of obligation which men break whenever it serves them, while fear is
held by a dread of punishment that never fails. Hobbes's
[Chapter 13](/chapters/13-of-the-naturall-condition-of-mankind/) reaches the
same place by a different road, and his sovereign exists precisely to supply
the dread that never fails.

They agree, too, that politics should be described rather than praised.
Both refuse to begin from the best regime, and both were hated for it.

### Where they part

On what to do about it. Machiavelli's admiration goes to the Roman republic,
to civic virtù, to a citizenry armed and quarrelsome enough to keep its
liberty — the *Discourses* argue that the tumults between senate and plebs
were the cause of Rome's greatness. For Hobbes that is a description of a
commonwealth dissolving, and the classical education that taught Englishmen to
love it is on his list of the
[causes of the civil war](/themes/the-english-civil-war/).

The other difference is method. Machiavelli reasons from examples, and his
examples can be answered with other examples. Hobbes wanted a politics that
followed from definitions the way a theorem follows, so that no example could
touch it.

### Did Hobbes read him?

Almost certainly — he was unavoidable, and Hobbes spent three long visits in
Italy. He never names him, which is a silence worth noticing, and puts him
among the [surprising omissions](/thinkers/#surprising-omissions).
