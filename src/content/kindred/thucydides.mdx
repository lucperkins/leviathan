He is the only kindred spirit Hobbes spent years on. The
[translation of 1629](/works/thucydides/) was his first published book, and
the preface calls
[Thucydides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thucydides) the most politic
historiographer that ever writ. What follows in *Leviathan* twenty years later
was already in the Greek.

### Where they agree

Three things. First, the motives: the Athenians at Sparta say they hold their
empire from fear, honour and interest, which is the triad Hobbes puts at the
centre of [Chapter 13](/chapters/13-of-the-naturall-condition-of-mankind/) as
competition, diffidence and glory. Second, the behaviour of assemblies: the
Sicilian expedition is voted for by men who have been flattered into it, and
Hobbes's objection to democracy is that eloquence beats reckoning wherever a
crowd decides.

Third, and closest of all, what civil war does to language. Thucydides on the
revolution at Corcyra describes men changing the accepted meanings of words to
fit their actions — recklessness renamed as courage, prudence as cowardice.
Hobbes's whole apparatus of definitions is built against that, and his account
of names given "from the appetite of the speaker" is the same observation
turned into a theory of [good and evil](/concepts/good-and-evil/).

### Where they part

Thucydides has no doctrine. He shows a plague dissolving law and religion at
Athens and leaves the reader to draw the moral; Hobbes will not leave anything
to the reader. The Greek is an observer of a world he thinks is simply like
that. The Englishman thinks the condition is escapable, by an artifice, and
spends four hundred pages describing it.

### Did Hobbes read him?

He did more than read him: he learned Greek prose on him and made him speak
English. And he never names him in *Leviathan* — which puts the closest
influence on the book among its
[surprising omissions](/thinkers/#surprising-omissions).
