[Ockham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_of_Ockham) is an awkward kindred spirit, because Hobbes
spends Part IV attacking the schoolmen as a class and Ockham was one of them.
But the semantics of *Leviathan* is his.

### Where they agree

That universals are not things. For Ockham, only individuals exist; a
universal is a sign, a term standing for many particulars, and to treat it as
a further entity is a mistake about language. Hobbes states the same position
flatly in [Chapter 4](/chapters/04-of-speech/):

> there being nothing in the world Universall but Names; for the things named,
> are every one of them Individual and Singular.

Everything follows from it. If universals are names, then truth is a property
of propositions rather than of things, [reason](/themes/reason/) is reckoning
with names, and the schools' abstract essences are words that name nothing —
which is exactly the charge of
[Chapter 46](/chapters/46-of-darknesse-from-vain-philosophy-and-fabulous-traditions/).
They agree, too, on the politics that follows from a razor: Ockham spent his
last twenty years writing against the Pope's plenitude of power from the same
Munich court that sheltered [Marsilius](/kindred/marsilius-of-padua/).

### Where they part

Ockham is a nominalist who remains a theologian. Behind the names stands a God
whose will makes the moral law, and a natural law that binds regardless of any
covenant. Hobbes takes the semantics and drops the rest: his laws of nature
are theorems about self-preservation, and what makes them law in the strict
sense is a sovereign's command.

### Did Hobbes read him?

Probably not directly, and certainly not sympathetically; the nominalist
tradition reached him through the logic he was taught at Oxford and grumbled
about ever afterwards. He owes the schoolmen more than he admits, and this is
where the debt is.
