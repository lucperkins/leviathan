[Epicurus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicurus) taught that everything is
atoms moving in void, that the soul is bodily and ends, that the gods exist
but take no interest in us, and that fear of them is the great source of human
misery. [Lucretius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucretius) put the whole
system into Latin hexameters in
[*De rerum natura*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_rerum_natura) (*On the
Nature of Things*), which is how the seventeenth century read it.

### Where they agree

On the physics, almost entirely: body in motion is all there is, the soul goes
with the body, and an incorporeal substance is a contradiction. On religion,
closely: Lucretius derives the gods from fear and dreams, which is the
argument of [Chapter 12](/chapters/12-of-religion/) about the natural seed of
religion, and Hobbes's account of apparitions as decaying sense misread is
Epicurean in method if not in detail.

And on justice, exactly. The *Principal Doctrines* say that justice never
existed in itself, but is a contract arising among people in their dealings,
a pledge neither to harm nor be harmed; where no such contract was made, there
is nothing just or unjust. Set that beside
[Chapter 15](/chapters/15-of-other-lawes-of-nature/) — the definition of
injustice is the not performance of covenant — and beside
[Chapter 13](/chapters/13-of-the-naturall-condition-of-mankind/), where in the
condition of war nothing can be unjust. Lucretius even supplies the
prehistory: neighbours who could destroy each other agreeing not to.

### Where they part

On what it is all for. Epicurus has a highest good, tranquillity, and advises
withdrawing from public life to secure it; Hobbes denies there is any such
thing as a "Summum Bonum, (greatest good,) as is spoken of in the Books of the
old Morall Philosophers", and requires a sovereign rather than a garden. The
Epicurean escapes politics. The Hobbesian cannot.

### Did Hobbes read them?

Certainly Lucretius, who was in every gentleman's library, and Epicurus
through him and through
[Pierre Gassendi](/kindred/gassendi/), whose Christianised revival of atomism
was being written in the same Paris circle in the same years. Lucretius is
another of the [surprising omissions](/thinkers/#surprising-omissions):
never named, everywhere present.
