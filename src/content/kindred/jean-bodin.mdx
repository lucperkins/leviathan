[Bodin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Bodin) wrote *Les Six Livres de la République* (*The Six Books of the Commonwealth*) in 1576, four
years after the massacre of St Bartholomew's Day, against Protestant writers
arguing that subjects might resist a king who broke faith. He is the nearest
thing Hobbes has to a direct predecessor.

### Where they agree

On the definition, which is Bodin's invention: sovereignty is the absolute and
perpetual power of a commonwealth. Absolute, so not held on conditions;
perpetual, so not a commission that expires or can be revoked; and above all
one, since a power divided is no sovereignty at all. The chief mark of it is
making law without the consent of any greater, equal or lesser. Every clause
of that reappears in
[Chapter 18](/chapters/18-of-the-rights-of-soveraignes-by-institution/), and
the indivisibility is the argument Hobbes uses in
[Chapter 29](/chapters/29-of-those-things-that-weaken-or-tend-to-the-dissolution-of-a-common-wealth/)
against the mixed constitution — a kingdom divided in itself cannot stand.

### Where they part

On the limits. Bodin's sovereign is bound by the law of God and of nature, by
the fundamental laws of the realm, and may not take a subject's property
without consent; these are real restraints, and the whole point of the
definition is that a lawful king is not a tyrant. Hobbes removes all three.
The laws of nature bind the sovereign in conscience only, there are no
fundamental laws he cannot alter, and property exists because the sovereign
established it and lasts as long as he says.

They differ, too, in kind. Bodin is a lawyer arguing from the constitutions of
actual states, and he also wrote a treatise urging the prosecution of
witches. Hobbes is deducing.

### Did Hobbes read him?

Very probably. The *République* was in English from 1606 and its vocabulary
was standard in the debates Hobbes was writing into. He never names him —
another of the [surprising omissions](/thinkers/#surprising-omissions), and
the most surprising of all, since the doctrine is Bodin's.
