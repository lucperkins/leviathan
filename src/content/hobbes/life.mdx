Thomas Hobbes was born on 5 April 1588 at Westport, the parish adjoining
Malmesbury in Wiltshire. The Spanish fleet was expected that summer, and in
the Latin verse autobiography he wrote at eighty-four he made a joke of the
coincidence that has followed him ever since: his mother, hearing the rumour
of the Armada, gave birth to twins, himself and fear. He returned to the
thought often enough that it is fair to take it as more than a joke. The
argument of *Leviathan* begins from what men are afraid of.

### A father who ran away

His father, also Thomas, was vicar of Charlton and Westport, and by every
surviving account an ill-educated and quarrelsome man. He fell into a brawl
with another clergyman at his own church door, struck him, and fled the
county, abandoning his wife and three children. Hobbes was then a boy. The
family was rescued by his uncle Francis, a prosperous Malmesbury glover
without sons of his own, who paid for the boy's schooling and, later, for
Oxford.

The schooling was good. He learned Latin and Greek at a small school in
Westport kept by Robert Latimer, a young graduate who taught him well enough
that he is said to have turned Euripides's *Medea* into Latin verse before he
left. He went up to Magdalen Hall, Oxford, at about fifteen, and stayed some
five years. He seems to have disliked nearly everything about it. The
curriculum was scholastic logic and Aristotelian physics, and the mature
Hobbes wrote about that training with a contempt that never cooled: the whole
attack on the universities in Part IV of *Leviathan* is, among other things,
a settling of accounts with his own education.

### The Cavendish household

In 1608, at twenty, he entered the service of William Cavendish of Hardwick,
and that connection lasted, with one interruption, for the rest of his life.
He served three generations of the family as tutor, secretary, companion, man
of business, and finally as an honoured old gentleman living in the house. He
never married, held no university post, and belonged to no church living. He
was a gentleman's dependant, and the position gave him what he needed:
libraries, leisure, travel, introductions, and the protection of a great
family.

### Exile

The politics caught up with him in 1640. He had written a short treatise, the
[*Elements of Law*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Elements_of_Law,_Natural_and_Politic), arguing for undivided sovereign power, and had let it
circulate in manuscript just as the Short Parliament was quarrelling with the
King over exactly that question. Fearing that its arguments would make him a
target, he left for France in the autumn, and afterwards observed with some
pride that he was the first of all who fled. He stayed eleven years, most of
them in Paris, and it was there, among the exiled royalist court and the
mathematicians of Marin Mersenne's circle, that he wrote *Leviathan*.

He came home in the winter of 1651–52, after the book was printed in London.
The decision was characteristic and was held against him by both sides: the
royalists in Paris thought the book an argument for submitting to Cromwell,
and the returning author did submit, making his peace with the Council of
State and settling quietly in London.

### The last years

The Restoration of 1660 treated him better than he might have expected.
Charles II had been his mathematics pupil in exile, remembered him fondly,
gave him the run of the court and a pension of a hundred pounds a year, paid
irregularly. But the hostility of the clergy never let up, and after the
plague and the Fire of 1666 a committee of the Commons looking into a bill
against atheism and profaneness named *Leviathan* by title. Hobbes, then
seventy-eight, burned papers and was never again permitted to publish in
England on religion or politics.

He kept working anyway. In his eighties he translated the whole of Homer into
English verse, remarking that he did it because he had nothing else to do. He
died on 4 December 1679, at Hardwick Hall, in his ninety-second year, and was
buried in the parish church at Ault Hucknall in Derbyshire, where his
monument still is.
