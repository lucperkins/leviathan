More than half of *Leviathan* is about religion. Parts III and IV run from
Chapter XXXII to the end, and they are the reason the book was dangerous. A
reader who knows only the state of nature and the covenant has read the
shorter and less controversial half.

### What he professed

Hobbes conformed. He attended the Church of England, received the sacrament,
and described himself as a Christian throughout his life, including in the
years when saying so was the only thing standing between him and prosecution.
He was on friendly terms with clergymen, and on his deathbed, by the account
of those present, he took communion according to the Anglican rite.

### What he actually held

The trouble is that the content of the belief was strange enough to alarm
almost everybody.

- **God is corporeal.** Hobbes's materialism is total: whatever exists is
  body, and *incorporeal substance* is for him not a mystery but rather two
  words that cancel each other out. Pressed on this by Bishop John Bramhall,
  he did not retreat but concluded instead that God must be a corporeal
  spirit, infinitely pure and invisible. He thought this more reverent than
  the alternative, since it at least says something rather than nothing.
- **The soul is not naturally immortal.** He held that the Christian hope is
  the resurrection of the body at the last day, not the survival of an
  immaterial soul in the meantime, and that the punishment of the wicked is a
  second death rather than everlasting conscious torment. Purgatory, on this
  account, has nothing to stand on, and neither do the ghosts and spirits
  that he thought the schools had smuggled into Christianity out of
  paganism.
- **The creed is very short.** In Chapter XLIII the one article necessary for
  salvation is that Jesus is the Christ. Everything else may be believed, but
  no one is damned for getting it wrong.
- **The sovereign decides the rest.** Since faith cannot be compelled and
  scripture does not interpret itself, the public interpretation belongs to
  the civil sovereign, and the church has no coercive power of its own. This
  is the argument that occupies Chapter XLII and the reply to
  [Bellarmine](/interlocutors/bellarmine/).

Add to this his definition of religion in Chapter VI as fear of invisible
powers, which counts as religion when publicly allowed and superstition when
it is not, and the reaction becomes easy to understand.
The definition puts true and false religion on the same footing as far as the
psychology goes, and leaves the difference to be settled by public
authority.

### The charge of atheism

*Hobbism* entered the language as a byword for irreligion. In 1666 a
committee of the Commons considering a bill against atheism and profaneness
named *Leviathan* explicitly, and Hobbes, seventy-eight and frightened,
destroyed papers. He was never prosecuted, but he was never again licensed to
publish on religion in England. In 1683, four years after his death, the
University of Oxford condemned propositions drawn from his books and had them
burned in the Schools quadrangle.

### The unresolved question

Whether he believed any of it is still argued. On one reading he is a
sincere, if wildly heterodox, Christian: a materialist who thought the
scriptural evidence supported mortalism and a minimal creed, and who was
attacking clerical power rather than religion. On another he is an unbeliever
writing under constraint, using orthodox vocabulary to hollow out the thing
from inside, and the sheer volume of scriptural exegesis in Parts III and IV
is a demonstration that anyone's doctrine can be got out of the text.

The evidence genuinely underdetermines the answer. He never wrote anything
that a prosecutor could use, which is consistent with caution and with
belief alike. What can be said is that his religion, whatever its sincerity,
was of a piece with the rest of his philosophy: it admitted no incorporeal
substances, no authority that could not be traced to a definition or a
sovereign, and no mystery that excused an undefined word.
