The people in the [touchstones](/touchstones/) list are the ones Hobbes
argued with in *Leviathan*, most of them long dead when he wrote. This page is
about the living: the households he served, the circles he was admitted to,
and the men who spent the last twenty years of his life attacking him.

### The Cavendishes

Everything follows from one appointment. In 1608 he entered the service of
William Cavendish, later second Earl of Devonshire, as tutor and companion to
his son, and apart from the years of exile he stayed with the family for the
rest of his life. The post gave him a library, three continental tours, and
the freedom to read for twenty years without having to produce anything. It
also gave him a second, grander branch of the family: the
Duke of Newcastle, who commissioned work from him, and his brother
Sir Charles Cavendish, who was a serious mathematician and the hinge between
Hobbes and the mathematicians abroad.

The debt runs the other way too. He was a servant, and he knew it. What the
arrangement bought was a man who could be [ninety-one](/hobbes/life/) and
still writing, in a house he had lived in for most of a century.

### Bacon at Gorhambury

Before any of that, in the 1620s, he did a stint as amanuensis to
Francis Bacon, walking with him in the gardens at Gorhambury and taking down
his thoughts as they came, and translating some of the essays into Latin.
Aubrey says Bacon liked him better than the other secretaries because he
understood what he was writing down. Nothing in *Leviathan* is Baconian in
method — Hobbes wanted demonstration, not the collection of instances — but
the ambition to reorganise all of knowledge on a new plan is a thing they
share, and Hobbes met it first as a young man taking dictation.

### Mersenne's circle

The third tour, in the mid-1630s, put him among the natural philosophers
around Marin Mersenne in Paris, and that is where he stopped being a well-read
gentleman's servant and became a philosopher with correspondents. Mersenne
circulated his work, solicited his objections, and printed him. Through that
circle he came to Pierre Gassendi, the nearest thing he had to an ally;
Kenelm Digby, who sent him books;
Samuel de Sorbière, who translated [*De Cive*](/works/de-cive/) into French and promoted him
tirelessly; and René Descartes, with whom it went badly. Hobbes wrote the third
set of Objections to the *Meditations* in 1641, Descartes replied with visible
irritation, and the two men never got on. In 1636 the same tour took him to
the elderly Galileo outside Florence, which he counted the high point of his
education.

### Great Tew, and the war

At home in the 1630s the interesting company was at Great Tew, the house near
Oxford where Lucius Cary,
Viscount Falkland kept a kind of permanent seminar on religion and toleration.
William Chillingworth,
Edward Hyde, and Sidney Godolphin all belonged to it; Hobbes was known there
and read there. The war then killed or scattered most of them. Falkland rode
into the fire at Newbury in 1643 and Godolphin was shot at Chagford the same
year, and the two deaths stand behind the flat sentence in the
[Review and Conclusion](/chapters/a-review-and-conclusion/) about a man slain
in the public quarrel by an undiscerning hand.

In Paris he taught mathematics to the exiled Prince of Wales, later
Charles II, between 1646 and 1648. It is the single most useful thing that
ever happened to him. When *Leviathan* was denounced in the Commons in 1666
the King protected his old tutor, on the condition that he publish nothing
further on religion or politics in England.

### The men who ran English science

The last quarter of his life was spent quarrelling, and losing. In [*De Corpore*](/works/de-corpore/)
(1655) he claimed to have squared the circle;
John Wallis demolished it, and
Seth Ward, the Savilian professor of astronomy, joined in against his
philosophy. Both were defending the universities *Leviathan* had attacked, and
both outlived the argument.

The other front was experiment. When
Robert Boyle published the air-pump trials, Hobbes answered in 1661 that a
machine cannot settle a question of natural philosophy, because what an
experiment yields is a fact about an apparatus and what philosophy needs is a
demonstration from causes. He also denied that a vacuum had been made, which
was the weaker half of a serious point. He lost, comprehensively, and the
Royal Society never elected him: the most famous philosopher in England was
kept out of the national scientific body by men he had insulted.
Steven Shapin and Simon Schaffer made that quarrel the subject of
[*Leviathan and the Air-Pump*](/further-reading/#leviathan-and-the-air-pump) (1985),
on the argument that what was being settled was not one experiment but rather
who gets to say what counts as knowledge.

### Aubrey

The exception to all this bad temper was
John Aubrey, a fellow Wiltshireman forty years his junior, who liked him,
visited him, and wrote down what he was like. Almost everything personal that
is known about Hobbes comes from him, and it is dealt with on the page about
[his character](/hobbes/character/). Without Aubrey he would be a body of work
with a biography attached; with him he is a man who played tennis at
seventy-five and sang in bed for his lungs.
