We know more about Hobbes's habits than about those of almost any other
philosopher of his century, and for a single reason: John Aubrey came from
the same corner of Wiltshire, met him as a boy, stayed friendly with him for
forty years, and wrote him up at length in the notes now known as *Brief
Lives*. Nearly every anecdote in circulation traces back there, so it is
worth saying at the outset that Aubrey was a gossip and a magpie, delightful
and not always reliable.

### In person

He was over six feet tall and carried himself upright into old age. Aubrey
records a hazel eye that stayed lively to the very end, a face that reddened
readily, and a head bald on top, though he kept his hair at the sides. He
sat, as an old man, writing in a chamber with very few books in it. Aubrey,
who noticed such things, says he never saw above half a dozen there, and
reports Hobbes's own explanation of it: if he had read as much as other men
did, he would have known as little.

That is bravado, and not quite true; he read plenty. But it points at
something real about his method. He distrusted authority and citation as ways
of settling questions, and preferred to reason from definitions he had fixed
himself. The thin bookshelf was the working style of a man who thought most
learning was a hindrance.

### Habits

His regimen was disciplined and slightly eccentric. He walked hard enough
each morning to work up a sweat, then had himself rubbed down. He played
tennis into his mid-seventies. At night, alone in bed with the doors shut so
that nobody could hear, he sang, believing it good for his lungs and likely
to lengthen his life. He was abstemious about drink in his later decades,
which for the period was unusual enough to be worth Aubrey's notice.

Best of all, and most in character for the author of a book about reckoning:
he walked with a stick that had a pen and inkhorn set into the head, and
carried a notebook, so that a thought arriving on the road could be written
down before it escaped.

### Timid, cheerful, generous

Hobbes said of himself that [he and fear were born twins](/hobbes/life/), and his conduct bore
it out. He fled England in 1640 ahead of any actual danger; he burned papers
in 1666 at the first hint of prosecution; he disliked being alone in the
dark, and did not care for stories about ghosts. He was not embarrassed by
any of this. In a philosophy where the fear of violent death is the beginning
of political wisdom, a candid coward has an argument for himself.

Against that, the picture Aubrey gives is of an unusually good-humoured man:
witty, quick in company, fond of conversation, charitable to poor relations
and to strangers who asked, and better liked in person than his reputation
would suggest. Opponents called him the Monster of Malmesbury, and *Hobbism*
became a term of abuse for irreligion and self-interest; the man himself
seems to have been genial company who enjoyed an argument and did not hold
grudges outside of print.

Inside of print he held them for decades. The same man who was charming at
dinner spent twenty-five years refusing to concede a mathematical error to
John Wallis. Vanity about his own reasoning was his most consistent fault,
and it is the one Aubrey, who admired him, does not try to explain away.
