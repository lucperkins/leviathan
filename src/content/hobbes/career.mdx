Hobbes was a slow starter to a degree that is hard to overstate. He published
nothing of his own until he was past fifty. The first four decades of his
working life were spent as a gentleman's servant of a superior kind, reading,
travelling, and keeping accounts, and there is little sign in them of the
philosopher he became.

### Thucydides and Euclid

His first book, published in 1629 when he was forty-one, was a translation of
Thucydides's history of the Peloponnesian War, the first made into English
directly from the Greek. The choice of author was not neutral. Hobbes admired
Thucydides for showing what democratic assemblies do to a state at war, and
said as much in the preface. The lesson he drew from the ancients was the
opposite of the one his contemporaries drew.

Around the same time came the episode that changed his mind about what
knowledge is. John Aubrey, who knew him, tells it: Hobbes was in a
gentleman's library, found a copy of Euclid's *Elements* lying open at the
theorem of Pythagoras, read the proposition, and swore it was impossible. So
he read the proof, which sent him back to an earlier proposition, and that to
another, until the chain of demonstration convinced him. He was forty-two,
and he had discovered geometry. Everything afterwards is an attempt to do for
politics what Euclid had done for space: begin from definitions and let the
conclusions follow whether or not anyone likes them.

### Travel and the new science

Three long continental journeys with his pupils gave him the rest of his
education. On the last, in the mid-1630s, he met the mathematicians and
natural philosophers around Marin Mersenne in Paris, who put him in touch
with everything then happening in the new science, and in 1636 he visited the
elderly Galileo in Italy, under house arrest outside Florence. From that
world he took the conviction that everything whatever is body in motion, and
that explanation means showing how the motions produce the effect. Applied to
human beings, that programme is Part I of *Leviathan*.

### The political books

- **1640 — [*The Elements of Law*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Elements_of_Law,_Natural_and_Politic).** Circulated in manuscript, not printed. It
  contains the essentials: the state of nature, the covenant, undivided
  sovereignty. Its circulation is what sent him abroad.
- **1642 — [*De Cive*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Cive).** Printed privately in Paris, and the book that made his
  European reputation. A public edition followed in 1647.
- **1651 — *Leviathan*.** Written in English, for an English audience, in the
  middle of the interregnum, and unlike the others addressed to ordinary
  readers rather than to scholars. Its scale is the difference: Parts III and
  IV, on scripture and the church, are more than half the book, and they are
  what made it notorious.

He completed the philosophical system afterwards, publishing *De Corpore* in
1655 and *De Homine* in 1658, so that the trilogy on body, man, and citizen
was finished in reverse order, with its political conclusion twenty years
ahead of its physical foundation.

### The quarrel with the mathematicians

The other long labour of his old age was a disaster. In *De Corpore* he
claimed to have squared the circle. John Wallis, Savilian professor of
geometry at Oxford, demolished the proof, and Hobbes, who could not concede,
spent the better part of twenty-five years producing fresh constructions and
fresh pamphlets while Wallis dismantled each in turn. The dispute was not
purely mathematical: Wallis was a Presbyterian divine defending the
universities Hobbes had attacked, and both men knew it. But Hobbes was simply
wrong, publicly and repeatedly, in the one field where he had claimed
certainty, and it cost him.

### The end

After 1666 he was effectively silenced in England on his own subjects.
*Behemoth*, his history of the Civil War written around 1668, was refused a
licence by the King, and appeared only after his death. So he turned to
Homer, publishing the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* in English verse in 1675 and
1676, in his late eighties, and he was still writing in the year he died.
