Hobbes is not one of the American founding authorities, and the omission is
deliberate. The books that shaped the constitutional argument were Locke,
Montesquieu, Blackstone, Harrington and Hume; Hobbes appears in the pamphlet
literature of the 1770s and 1780s mainly as an epithet, the name you attached to
an opponent who seemed to think power made right. A republic being argued into
existence had little use for a philosopher whose central claim was that the
sovereign cannot be resisted.

He arrived a century and a half later, from Europe, and then several times over.

### The emigration

Leo Strauss came to the New School in 1938 and to Chicago in 1949, bringing the
Hobbes of his 1936 book: not a scientist of politics but a moralist, whose
system rests on the fear of violent death, and who is the founder of modernity
in the specific sense that he lowered what politics is for. Two generations of
American graduate students learned political philosophy as a story with Hobbes
at its hinge, and Harvey Mansfield and Joseph Cropsey kept him there. Whatever
one thinks of the reading, it is the reason Hobbes is taught in the United
States at all.

Hannah Arendt published the opposite verdict in 1951 and in the same
émigré register. Her Hobbes is the philosopher of the bourgeoisie: an
accumulator whose commonwealth has no principle of limit, whose subjects have
interests but no public life, and whose logic runs out in imperial expansion.
It is not scholarship and does not pretend to be, and it has probably been read
by more Americans than any monograph on the subject.

### The analytic reconstruction

The distinctively American contribution came later and looks nothing like
either. Its method is to take the argument apart and ask whether it works.

[David Gauthier's](/further-reading/#gauthier-logic) *The Logic of Leviathan*
(1969) set the pattern, and Gregory Kavka and Jean Hampton carried it through
in the 1980s. The state of nature became a collective action problem, the
Foole of Chapter 15 became the free rider, and the question stopped being what
Hobbes meant and became whether a rational agent would do what he says.
Hampton's answer — that the sovereign cannot be produced by the covenant that
requires him — is the sharpest objection the tradition has generated.

John Rawls sits oddly in this. *A Theory of Justice* explicitly builds on
Locke, Rousseau and Kant and puts *Leviathan* aside; his lectures, delivered
for years at Harvard, begin with Hobbes and give him more time than anyone.
Robert Nozick did the more Hobbesian thing without saying so, deriving a state
from competing protective agencies with nobody ever agreeing to anything.

### Strategy, and the states system

The Cold War made the state of nature a working model. Game theory arrived at
RAND with the prisoner's dilemma in 1950, and the resemblance to Chapter 13 was
noticed at once; James Buchanan built
[constitutional economics](/ancestor/#public-choice) on an explicitly Hobbesian
account of rules chosen in advance by parties who cannot trust each other.

The largest institutional debt is in international relations. Kenneth Waltz
made anarchy — no common power above the parties — the structural fact from
which everything else follows. It is Chapter 13's last paragraph, where
Hobbes says that kings, because of their independency, are in the posture of
gladiators, turned into a research programme. American IR spent forty years
arguing about whether that posture is as determining as he said, which is a
better tribute than agreement.

### The undergraduate Hobbes

The result is a country where he is simultaneously marginal and inescapable.
No American constitutional argument cites him. Every introductory course puts
him in the second week, between Plato and Locke, as the man who explained why
there is a state at all — nasty, brutish and short, three words that most
people who can produce them have never read in context. It is a thinner Hobbes
than the British or German traditions carry, and it has reached far more
people.
