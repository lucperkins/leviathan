Catholic Europe read Hobbes through his refutations. The Holy Office condemned
him, his books went on the Index, and for most of the eighteenth century an
Italian reader met him as a set of propositions in somebody else's chapter of
errors. That is a poor start, and it makes the twentieth century stranger:
Italy has produced more original work on Hobbes since 1950 than any country
except Germany.

### Vico

The one substantial early response comes from Naples, and it is not really a
response so much as a parallel objection made by a man working alone.
Giambattista Vico complains that the natural law writers — Hobbes among them,
and Grotius and Pufendorf and Selden alongside — begin from a man who is
already the product of centuries of civil life, endowed with foresight,
calculation and a sense of his own advantage, and then present that man as
nature's. Real first men were brutes governed by fear of thunder and by
imagination, and what they built was not a covenant but a religion.

The charge is Rousseau's, three decades early, in a book almost nobody read
until the nineteenth century.

### Bobbio

Modern Italian Hobbes begins with Norberto Bobbio, and it begins as a question
about law. Bobbio spent a career on the argument between natural law and legal
positivism, and he put Hobbes at the point where they meet: a writer who
derives laws of nature by reason and then denies that they are law at all until
a sovereign commands them. Most traditions treat that as an inconsistency to be
resolved in one direction or the other. Bobbio treated it as the structure of
the problem itself, and used it — in a country rebuilding its constitution after
fascism — to ask what obedience to law can rest on when it cannot rest on the
law being good.

That is the Italian tone throughout. Hobbes is not a curiosity of the
seventeenth century but a live question about what a state is entitled to.

### The sovereignty theorists

Carl Schmitt's Hobbes reached Italy after the war largely through Carlo Galli
and was taken up rather than quarantined, which is part of why the recent work
looks the way it does. Three lines came out of it.

Giorgio Agamben reads the state of nature backwards. It is not a condition the
commonwealth leaves behind but one it keeps inside itself and can produce at
will: the state of exception, where law is suspended by law, and the figure it
produces is the man who may be killed without it counting as a crime. Hobbes's
wolf becomes the wolf-man of the ban, and Chapter 28's account of what a
sovereign may do to those outside the covenant becomes the centre of the book
rather than a technicality.

Roberto Esposito turns the same material into a theory of immunity. A community
is a set of obligations people owe each other; Hobbes's remedy for the danger
those bonds create is to cut them and route everything through the sovereign, so
that people are protected from one another by being separated from one another.
The commonwealth is an inoculation, and like an inoculation it works by
introducing what it defends against.

Antonio Negri, with Michael Hardt, takes the one term Hobbes needed to
neutralise and makes it the protagonist. The multitude that becomes a people by
authorising a representative is, for Hobbes, the moment politics begins; for
Negri it is the moment it is captured, and the multitude that refuses to be
represented is what has to be thought instead. It is Chapter 16 read as a crime
scene.

None of this is what Hobbes thought he was writing, and none of it is
inattentive to what he wrote. Italy has been unusually willing to take the
strangest passages — the ban, the enemy outside the covenant, the multitude
before it is a person — as the ones that matter.
