*Leviathan* was published in London in 1651 into a country that had executed
its king two years earlier, and its first readers were not scholars but people
deciding whether to swear an oath to the new regime. That is the reception the
book got, and it never quite escaped it.

### Hobbism

The word arrives almost at once, and it does not mean a doctrine so much as a
character: a Hobbist was an atheist, a scoffer, a man who thought self-interest
went all the way down and said so at dinner. Restoration comedy has the type
ready-made. The label attached itself to courtiers, libertines and anyone who
argued that religion was useful rather than true, most of whom had not read a
page of him.

The official responses followed the label. After the plague and the fire, the
Commons in October 1666 ordered a committee to prepare a bill against atheism
and profaneness and empowered it to receive information about *Leviathan* in
particular. Nothing came of the bill, but Hobbes burned papers, and never
published on religion in England again. In 1683 the University of Oxford
condemned a list of pernicious propositions and had the offending books burned
in the Schools quadrangle; Hobbes was on it, alongside Milton and Buchanan, in
what was less a philosophical judgement than a loyalty display in the middle of
the exclusion crisis.

### The answerers

They were numerous, and Samuel Mintz's survey found that most of them went
after the materialism rather than the politics: it is easier to prosecute a man
for saying the soul is a body than for saying subjects must obey.

The interesting ones went after the politics anyway, and from every direction.
Robert Filmer got there first, in 1652, and his objection is the one that
stings: he grants Hobbes everything about what a sovereign may do and rejects
the covenant entirely, because a story in which men make themselves subjects by
agreement gives away in its first move the thing absolutism most needs, that
authority was never theirs to give. James Harrington, from the republican side,
thought the whole apparatus beside the point — power in a country follows the
ownership of land, and no covenant will hold a state together whose property
has shifted underneath it — and said in the same breath that Hobbes was the
finest writer then living. Edward Hyde, Earl of Clarendon, wrote as an old
acquaintance and a wounded royalist: the doctrine that allegiance follows
protection had told Englishmen they might submit to whoever held the country,
which was, in 1651, an argument for Cromwell.

[John Bramhall](/works/of-liberty-and-necessity/) had been arguing with him
about the will since 1645, and *The Catching of Leviathan* in 1658 turned the
quarrel on the whole book.

### Locke's silence

John Locke does not name Hobbes in the *Two Treatises of Government*. The
*First Treatise* demolishes Filmer at length; the *Second* builds a state of
nature that is not a state of war, a natural law that binds before any
sovereign exists, and a right of resistance that Hobbes had spent Chapter 21
making unavailable. Whether the target is Hobbes, or Filmer, or the general
absolutist case that both had made in different ways, is an argument that has
run for a century of scholarship. What is not in doubt is that the English
tradition after 1690 took the Lockean version and left the other one on the
shelf, which is why the country that produced Hobbes has a constitutional
history written almost entirely without him.

### The utilitarian rescue

He came back through the lawyers. Bentham and then John Austin took the
command theory of law — law is the will of a sovereign, addressed to subjects
who are in the habit of obeying, backed by a threat — and made it the
foundation of English jurisprudence, without the covenant, the state of nature
or God. It is Chapter 26 turned into a working professional doctrine, and it
gave Hobbes a place in a living discipline at exactly the moment his politics
had none.

The scholarly rehabilitation came with the money of a radical MP:
[Sir William Molesworth](/further-reading/#molesworth-works) paid for and
edited the eleven volumes of English works and five of Latin between 1839 and
1845, which made the whole of Hobbes available for the first time.
Leslie Stephen wrote the Victorian verdict at its most characteristic in
1904: the prose is magnificent, the conclusions are appalling, and the man is
best understood as an eccentric of genius.

### The twentieth century, twice

Britain then produced two Hobbeses, in the same universities, forty years
apart.

The first was philosophical. [Michael Oakeshott](/further-reading/#oakeshott-introduction)
introduced the Blackwell edition of 1946 by declaring *Leviathan* the greatest,
perhaps the sole, masterpiece of political philosophy written in English, and
argued that its subject is authority rather than power and that the will it
describes is individual all the way down.
[Howard Warrender](/further-reading/#warrender-obligation) followed in 1957 with
the case that obligation rests on the laws of nature as God's commands, which
set off the Taylor–Warrender controversy and occupied the journals for twenty
years.

The second was historical. [Quentin Skinner](/readings/contextualist/) and the
Cambridge school insisted that the book is a move in the arguments of its own
moment — the engagement controversy, the humanist training in rhetoric, the
republican theory of liberty it was written to destroy — and that reading it as
a timeless system is reading it wrongly.
[Noel Malcolm](/further-reading/#malcolm-leviathan) completed the Clarendon
edition in 2012, with the English and Latin on facing pages, and settled a
great many questions of fact about how the book was made.

Between them they turned the national scandal into a national possession. The
country that burned him at Oxford now prints him with an apparatus longer than
the text.
