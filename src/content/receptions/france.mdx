Hobbes arrived in Paris in November 1640, eleven days after the Long Parliament
met, and stayed until the winter of 1651. *De Cive* was printed there in 1642,
*Leviathan* was written there, and the objections that made his philosophical
name were solicited there. Whatever else the French reception is, it began as a
conversation with the man himself.

### The Paris decade

[Marin Mersenne](/hobbes/contemporaries/) ran the circle, and it was Mersenne
who put Hobbes's objections in front of
[Descartes](/hobbes/contemporaries/) with the *Meditations* in 1641. The
exchange is famous for its coldness. Hobbes objects that thinking things need
not be immaterial, that a thing that thinks might be corporeal for all the
argument shows; Descartes replies with visible contempt and afterwards refused
to correspond. Neither man moved an inch, and the encounter is the cleanest
statement anywhere of what was at stake between them.

[Pierre Gassendi](/kindred/gassendi/) was the friend rather than the antagonist:
a priest reviving Epicurus, doing much the same work on sense and atoms, and
keeping the immaterial soul that Hobbes would not. Samuel Sorbière translated
*De Cive* into French in 1649 and spent years promoting him on the continent,
which is why Hobbes was read in Europe as the author of *De Cive* long before
anyone there read *Leviathan*.

### The Enlightenment's useful enemy

The eighteenth century made him the position you refuted on your way to your
own. Pierre Bayle's dictionary article is unusually generous.
Montesquieu opens *De l'esprit des lois* by taking the state of nature apart
in a page: fear would make natural man flee, not attack, and the will to
dominate is a thing society teaches. Diderot wrote the *Encyclopédie* article
and set the system out at length before rejecting it.

The sustained attack is [Rousseau's](/after-hobbes/#rousseau-inequality). The
*Discourse on Inequality* concedes that Hobbes reasoned correctly and denies
his premise: what he described was not natural man but civilised man with the
institutions taken away, a creature already formed by comparison, vanity and
property, and then projected backwards into the woods. It is the objection that
has been made to Hobbes more often than any other, and Rousseau made it best.

### The French Hobbes, as a scholarly object

Modern French Hobbes studies begin with Raymond Polin in 1953 and become a
field with François Tricaud, whose 1971 translation is the version French
readers mean when they say *Léviathan*. Yves Charles Zarka has led it since,
with Luc Foisneau on divine omnipotence and Franck Lessay on the writings about
religion. It is a tradition unusually attentive to the metaphysics — to what
Hobbes thinks there is, before any question of what men should do about it.

### The twentieth century's adversary

The interesting French engagement, though, is not scholarship but opposition,
and it is remarkably consistent: each of them treats the covenant as a story
that conceals how power actually operates.

[Michel Foucault](/after-hobbes/#foucault-society-must-be-defended), in the
1976 lectures, argues that Hobbes's war of all against all is a theatre of
representations rather than a real war, and that its purpose was to eliminate a
rival account then circulating in England — the historical discourse of
conquest, of Normans and Saxons, of a society permanently divided by a war that
never ended. Hobbes, on this reading, invented the state of nature precisely so
that history could be excluded from politics.

Pierre Clastres attacks the same point anthropologically. Societies without
states are not waiting for a sovereign; they have chiefs without coercive
power, and institutions that keep power from accumulating. The state is not
what happens when a multitude gets sensible. It is what happens when those
defences fail.

Jacques Derrida spent two years of seminar, published as
[*The Beast and the Sovereign*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Beast_and_the_Sovereign),
on the sovereign as an animal figure, and much of the first volume is on
Hobbes: on the frontispiece, on the wolf of *De Cive*, and on the strangeness
of an artificial man who is also a sea monster and a mortal god.
Louis Althusser had lectured on him in the same building a generation earlier.
Bruno Latour turned him into one half of the modern constitution.

Gilles Deleuze is the exception that proves how this works. He wrote nothing on
Hobbes, and reaches him only through Spinoza, for whom the difference is that
natural right survives into the civil state as power rather than being handed
over. The resemblance between Hobbes's restless desire and the desiring-machines
of *Anti-Oedipus* is real, and it is a
[coincidence of structure](/concepts/desire/) rather than a debt.
