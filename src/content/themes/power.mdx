Power is the hinge on which *Leviathan* turns. It is defined early, in the
middle of the account of human nature, and everything afterwards — competition,
war, the covenant, sovereignty, even the quarrel with Rome — is a consequence of
what the definition says. Hobbes gives it in a single line:

> The POWER of a Man, (to take it Universally,) is his present means, to obtain
> some future apparent Good. And is either Originall, or Instrumentall.
>
> — Chapter X

Three things are packed into that sentence. Power is *means*, not achievement:
it is what you can do, not what you have done. It is oriented to the *future*,
so a man's power is measured by what is still ahead of him. And the good it
aims at is *apparent*, the good as it seems to the man himself, since Hobbes
has already argued in Chapter VI that there is no common object all men call
good.

### Natural and instrumental

The division is between what a person is and what a person has got. Natural
powers are faculties of body and mind: strength, form, prudence, eloquence.
Instrumental powers are the ones acquired by those, or by luck, and they are
means to acquire still more:

> Instrumentall are those Powers, which acquired by these, or by fortune, are
> means and Instruments to acquire more: as Riches, Reputation, Friends, and
> the Secret working of God, which men call Good Luck.
>
> — Chapter X

The clause that matters is *means and Instruments to acquire more*. Power
compounds. Hobbes reaches for two images at once, fame and falling bodies,
both of which gather speed as they go. This is why the pursuit of power has no
natural resting place: each increment makes the next one easier, and nobody
who stops is safe from those who did not.

### Power is comparative, and therefore social

Because power is a *means* to future goods, and because men compete for the
same goods, one man's power is only ever measured against another's. Hobbes
draws the consequences relentlessly. Worth is not intrinsic but a price: "so
much as would be given for the use of his Power," and, like any price, set by
the buyer rather than the seller. Honour is the acknowledgement of power, and
dishonour its denial. Dignity is the public rate the common-wealth sets on a
man. Even belief in power produces the thing itself:

> Reputation of power, is Power; because it draweth with it the adhaerance of
> those that need protection.
>
> — Chapter X

That last move is characteristic. Once power is defined as means, anything that
functions as a means counts — a reputation, a friendship, a rumour, a doctrine.
This is what lets Hobbes treat sword and pulpit as instances of one kind of
thing in Part III.

### The restless desire

Chapter XI states the psychological conclusion. Only the description of the
state of nature — *solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short* — is quoted more
often:

> a generall inclination of all mankind, a perpetuall and restlesse desire of
> Power after power, that ceaseth onely in Death.
>
> — Chapter XI

Hobbes immediately guards against a misreading. This is not the claim that
everyone is greedy, or that each man wants ever more intense delight. The
reason is defensive: a man "cannot assure the power and means to live well,
which he hath present, without the acquisition of more." Even the moderate man
must keep acquiring, because the immoderate will not stop. Insecurity, not
appetite, drives the escalation.

### Why a common power is necessary

The argument of Chapter XIII follows directly. Men are roughly equal in
natural power — the weakest can kill the strongest — so equality of ability
produces equality of hope, and equality of hope produces competition. Nothing
in the situation itself sets a limit. The result is the famous condition:

> during the time men live without a common Power to keep them all in awe,
> they are in that condition which is called Warre; and such a warre, as is of
> every man, against every man.
>
> — Chapter XIII

Note that what is missing is named as a *power*, not a law or an agreement.
Hobbes has already said that covenants without the sword are but words. The
remedy therefore has to be a power greater than any individual's, and Chapter
XVII says exactly how such a thing can exist: not by adding powers together
loosely, as in a faction or a league, but by making them all depend on one
will.

> The Greatest of humane Powers, is that which is compounded of the Powers of
> most men, united by consent, in one person, Naturall, or civill, that has the
> use of all their Powers depending on his will; such as is the Power of a
> Common-wealth.
>
> — Chapter X

The commonwealth, on this account, is not a new kind of thing at all. It is
the same quantity, power, gathered into a single point. That is what the
frontispiece draws: a giant whose body is the multitude, holding the means of
coercion in his hands.

### Power ecclesiastical

Part III applies the definition to the church, and the definition does most of
the work. If power is the means to obtain a future good, then the question
about any bishop, council, or pope is simply: what means do they actually
hold? Hobbes's answer in Chapter XLII is that the apostolic commission
conveyed the power to teach and to persuade, which is not government, since it
carries no means of compelling anyone. Jurisdiction, by contrast, "is the
Power of hearing and determining Causes between man and man," and belongs only
to whoever can make law and enforce it with the sword. There is no third
category. A spiritual power that could command a sovereign would have to be a
civil power under another name — which is precisely what Hobbes accuses
[Bellarmine](/touchstones/bellarmine/) of smuggling in under the word
*indirectly*.
