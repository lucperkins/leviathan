*Leviathan* is built around one fear. Hobbes had a famous joke about it: born
prematurely in 1588 when his mother heard rumours of the Spanish Armada, he
said that "fear and I were born twins." The book's most quoted sentence is a
description of what life is like when that fear has nothing to check it.

### The war of all against all

Chapter XIII argues that without a common power to hold everyone in awe,
people are in a condition of war. Not necessarily fighting, but rather always ready
to, the way bad weather is not one shower but rather a disposition to rain. Three
things bring it about: competition for scarce goods, distrust that leads to
striking first, and the desire for glory. Hobbes's description of the result
is the passage everyone remembers:

> In such condition, there is no place for Industry; because the fruit
> thereof is uncertain: and consequently no Culture of the Earth; no
> Navigation, nor use of the commodities that may be imported by Sea; no
> commodious Building; no Instruments of moving, and removing such things as
> require much force; no Knowledge of the face of the Earth; no account of
> Time; no Arts; no Letters; no Society; and which is worst of all,
> continuall feare, and danger of violent death; And the life of man,
> solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short.
>
> — Chapter XIII

The crucial phrase is "danger of violent death." Hobbes does not say the
state of nature is unpleasant; he says it is deadly, and deadly for everyone,
because even the strongest can be killed in their sleep or by confederacy.
Rough equality in the capacity to kill is what makes the condition a war
rather than a hierarchy.

### Fear as a rational passion

Hobbes then turns the fear around. The same passion that makes the natural
condition intolerable is what makes people leave it:

> The Passions that encline men to Peace, are Feare of Death; Desire of such
> things as are necessary to commodious living; and a Hope by their Industry
> to obtain them.
>
> — Chapter XIII

This is why Hobbes can be both a pessimist about human nature and an optimist
about politics. People do not need to become good to build a state; they need
only to be frightened, and to reason correctly about what they are frightened
of.

### The sword behind the covenant

Violence does not disappear once the state exists. It is concentrated. The
laws of nature in Chapters XIV and XV tell people to seek peace, to keep their
agreements, to be accommodating, and so on, but these laws bind only "in
conscience" until someone can enforce them. Hobbes puts it flatly at the
start of Chapter XVII:

> Covenants, without the Sword, are but Words, and of no strength to secure a
> man at all.
>
> — Chapter XVII

The sovereign is the sword. What the subjects have done is transfer their
private rights of violence to one public holder of them, so that keeping
one's word becomes safer than breaking it. Punishment, treated in Chapter
XXVIII, is the state's use of this reserved violence, and Hobbes is careful to
argue that it is legitimate only when it serves to deter future harm, not as
revenge.

### The limits

There is one thing the state's violence cannot touch. The right to resist
one's own death was never transferred, because it could not be: the only
reason anyone submitted was to stay alive. So a condemned prisoner may
lawfully resist the executioner, a soldier may lawfully flee (though he may be
punished for it), and no one is obliged to testify against themselves. Hobbes
is not squeamish about the state's use of force, but he is precise about what
it is for.
