Readers who stop at the end of Part II miss half the book. Parts III ("Of a
Christian Common-wealth") and IV ("Of the Kingdome of Darknesse") are as long
as Parts I and II together, and to Hobbes's contemporaries they were the most
provocative half. The problem they address is simple to state. Hobbes has
argued that subjects owe obedience to the sovereign. But what if God commands
otherwise? A Christian who believes their salvation depends on disobeying will
disobey, and a state whose subjects believe that is not secure.

### Religion as a natural phenomenon

Hobbes prepares the ground early. Chapter II explains dreams and visions as
products of the body; the end of Chapter XI and the whole of Chapter XII,
"Of Religion," derive religion itself from human nature. People want to know
the causes of things, they fear what they cannot see, and they fill the gap
with invisible agents:

> And this Feare of things invisible, is the naturall Seed of that, which
> every one in himself calleth Religion; and in them that worship, or feare
> that Power otherwise than they do, Superstition.

This is not an argument against Christianity. Hobbes distinguishes the
natural seed of religion, common to all peoples, from true religion, which
comes by revelation. But it does establish that religious belief is something
a sovereign has to manage, because it grows in the same soil as superstition
and can be cultivated by anyone who wants power.

### What scripture actually says

Part III is a long exercise in biblical interpretation, and Hobbes's method is
his usual one: define the terms and see what follows. The results are
deflationary. The "Kingdome of God" in scripture, he argues, is not the church
or a spiritual realm but a literal civil kingdom: the one God held over the
Israelites, and the one Christ will restore at his second coming. Until then,
there is no Kingdom of God on earth, so no one can rule in its name. "Spirit"
and "angel" usually mean wind, breath, or a messenger, not an incorporeal
substance. Miracles ceased with the apostles. Eternal life and hell are not
what the schools teach. And the whole of Christian faith reduces to a single
article:

> the Spirit of unfained Christianity, or Submission to that main Article of
> Christian faith, that Jesus is the Christ
>
> — Chapter XXXIV

Everything else, in Hobbes's view, is a matter on which the sovereign may
lawfully decide what is publicly taught. A subject who inwardly believes
otherwise is not required to say so; faith is internal, and only outward
profession can be commanded.

### The sovereign as head of the church

The conclusion of Part III is that in a Christian commonwealth the sovereign
is also the supreme pastor. There cannot be two authorities, one civil and one
spiritual, because the subjects would then have two masters and the state
would be divided against itself. Hobbes finds this in scripture (kings of
Israel exercised priestly authority) and in reason (whoever can command
belief can command rebellion). The Church of England's royal supremacy is,
on this reading, not a compromise but rather the correct arrangement, and any church
that claims independence of the civil power is a threat.

### Rome

The rival supremacy is Rome's, and answering it takes more room than any other
single argument in the book. Chapter XLII, the longest chapter in *Leviathan*,
gives its last third to [Cardinal Bellarmine](/touchstones/bellarmine/),
book by book and text by text: the most sustained reply to a named opponent
anywhere in Hobbes. The question was not an academic one. After the Gunpowder
Plot, James I had required his Catholic subjects to swear that the Pope could
not depose a king; Bellarmine answered the King in print, the King answered
back, and every English reader knew what was at issue.

Bellarmine's position was the careful one, which is what makes it worth the
space. The Pope claims no direct temporal authority over princes, only an
indirect authority: power over their temporal affairs in order to the
spiritual good, to be used when souls require it. Hobbes will not have the
distinction at any price.

> For this distinction of Temporall, and Spirituall Power is but words. Power
> is as really divided, and as dangerously to all purposes, by sharing with
> another Indirect Power, as with a Direct one.

His answer to the claim is not to move the power to the king. It is to deny
that there is any such power to move. Pastors teach, and teaching is not
commanding. The church is not a kingdom in this world, because Christ's
kingdom begins at his second coming. Excommunication binds nobody the civil
sovereign has not obliged to be bound, and a king excommunicated is simply a
king. There is no church capable of willing or commanding anything at all
unless the sovereign is its head. Take those away and nothing is left for the
papal claim to rest on: not infallibility, not the exemption of the clergy
from the civil law, and above all not the power to release subjects from their
allegiance.

Part IV then asks how so large a claim was built, and answers with an
inventory. Chapter XLVII goes through the doctrines that pay — the
sacramentation of marriage and the single life of priests, auricular
confession, canonisation of saints, transubstantiation, purgatory and
indulgences, the exemption of the clergy, the school divinity that supplies
the vocabulary for all of it — and reads each the way a lawyer reads a motive:
whoever receives the benefit is presumed to be the author. Then the sentence
the chapter is remembered for:

> And if a man consider the originall of this great Ecclesiasticall Dominion,
> he will easily perceive, that the Papacy, is no other, than the Ghost of the
> deceased Romane Empire, sitting crowned upon the grave thereof.

It would be a mistake to read this as ordinary Protestant polemic. The
doctrine that the church militant is the kingdom of God was taught first at
Rome, but the Presbytery kept it after forbidding much else, and for the same
reason: "The same which the Popes expected: to have a Soveraign Power over the
People." The ministers who had just fought a war in England are charged with
the papal offence on the papal grounds. What Hobbes is against is not the
Catholic religion but rather any spiritual authority standing outside the
commonwealth that can reach subjects over the sovereign's head.

The circumstances sharpen it. He wrote all of this in Paris, in the eleventh
year of [an exile](/hobbes/life/#exile) spent with the royalist court, under
the protection of a Catholic queen and among Catholic clergy, and he went home
in the winter of 1651–52 with the book printed in London.

### The kingdom of darkness

Part IV asks where the contrary doctrines came from, and answers with a
history. The "Kingdome of Darknesse" is a confederacy of deceivers who
misinterpret scripture to gain power over people. Its instruments are the
doctrine that the church is the Kingdom of God, the vain philosophy of
Aristotle absorbed into theology, and the traditions of pagan religion
surviving under new names. Hobbes's target is above all the Papacy, whose
hierarchy he compares to "the Kingdome of Fairies," but the Presbyterians who
had fought the civil war come in for the same treatment. Both, he argues,
claim a spiritual authority that no scripture grants and that no state can
tolerate.

### Was Hobbes a Christian?

Contemporaries called him an atheist, and the charge has never quite gone
away. What is clear is that he professed Christianity, took enormous trouble
over scripture, and built his argument so that a believing Christian could
accept it. What is also clear is that the God of *Leviathan* is a body, that
its heaven is on earth, and that its church is a department of state. Whether
that is a reformed Christianity or a dismantled one is a question readers have
argued about since 1651.
