Because Hobbes builds the mind out of motion, the faculties he describes in
Part I belong to any animal with a nervous system, and he says so as he goes.
Imagination is decaying sense, "and is found in men, and many other living
Creatures, as well sleeping, as waking." Memory is the same thing under
another name, and much memory is experience. Prudence, the presumption of the
future from the past, is available to anything that can remember; a beast that
has learned where the danger is has it.

Even understanding, in one sense, is not peculiar to us. Hobbes allows in
Chapter II that a dog understands the call and the rating of his master, since
understanding is nothing but imagination raised by signs. What the dog cannot
do is understand a sentence.

### What the line is

The break comes at speech, and it is the only break that matters. Names are
arbitrary marks, imposed by us and settled by agreement, and everything Hobbes
builds afterwards runs through them. Reason is reckoning with names, so no
creature without names can reason. Science is the record of that reckoning.
Truth is a property of ordered names, so there is no truth in a world without
speech, and no falsehood either.

Religion follows the same rule, and Hobbes uses animals to prove it. Seeing no
sign or fruit of religion in any creature but man, he concludes the seed of it
must lie in something peculiar to man — the search for causes, and the anxiety
about time to come, which are functions of the same faculty that produces
names.

### Bees and ants

The animal argument reaches its point in
[Chapter XVII](/chapters/17-of-the-causes-generation-and-definition-of-a-common-wealth/),
where Hobbes has to answer an obvious objection. If men need an absolute
sovereign to live together, why do social insects manage without one? He grants
the case fully, and notes whose case it is: bees and ants live sociably,
"which are therefore by Aristotle numbred amongst Politicall creatures," with
no direction beyond their own appetites.

Six differences follow, and every one of them is a consequence of language.
Men compete for honour and dignity, which insects do not, so envy and hatred
and finally war arise among men alone. Men distinguish the common good from
the private. Men can find fault with the administration and think themselves
wiser than those who govern. Men have speech, and can therefore represent good
as evil and evil as good, discontenting others at will. And men distinguish
injury from damage, so they are most troublesome when most at ease. Then the
conclusion:

> Lastly, the agreement of these creatures is Naturall; that of men, is by
> Covenant only, which is Artificiall: and therefore it is no wonder if there
> be somewhat else required (besides Covenant) to make their Agreement constant
> and lasting; which is a Common Power, to keep them in awe.
>
> — Chapter XVII

The comparison is doing more work than it looks. Aristotle had called man a
political animal, and the bee argument was his evidence that political life is
natural. Hobbes concedes the bees and takes the conclusion away: their concord
is natural precisely because it is not political, and ours has to be
manufactured because we can talk.

### The beast on the title page

There is one more animal in the book, and it is the largest. The commonwealth
takes its name from the sea monster of Job, described in the verse that runs
across the top of the frontispiece: there is no power on earth to be compared
to him. The state Hobbes designs is an artificial animal, made of the men who
compose it, and the point of the image is that nothing natural is big enough
to do the job.
