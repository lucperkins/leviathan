The ["naturall condition of mankind"](/chapters/13-of-the-naturall-condition-of-mankind/) is the hinge of *Leviathan*. Everything
before Chapter XIII describes what people are like; everything after it
describes what they must build. The chapter in between asks what would happen
if people with exactly those natures were left entirely to themselves.

### Equality

The argument begins from an unexpected premise. Hobbes does not say people are
naturally unequal and need a ruler to keep the weak in line. He says they are
naturally *equal*, and that this is the problem:

> Nature hath made men so equall, in the faculties of body, and mind; as that
> though there bee found one man sometimes manifestly stronger in body, or of
> quicker mind then another; yet when all is reckoned together, the
> difference between man, and man, is not so considerable, as that one man
> can thereupon claim to himselfe any benefit, to which another may not
> pretend, as well as he.
>
> — Chapter XIII

The weakest can kill the strongest by stealth or by joining with others. And
in matters of the mind, everyone thinks themselves at least as wise as
everyone else, which Hobbes takes as good evidence that they roughly are.
Equality of ability produces equality of hope, and equality of hope produces
conflict whenever two people want the same thing.

### Three causes of quarrel

Hobbes finds three sources of conflict in human nature: competition, which
makes people attack for gain; diffidence (distrust), which makes them attack
for safety, since striking first is the rational response to a threat one
cannot otherwise remove; and glory, which makes them attack over slights to
their reputation. None of these requires people to be wicked. They require
only that people want things, fear each other, and care what others think of
them.

### War

The result is a condition Hobbes calls war, and he is careful about what he
means by the word:

> Hereby it is manifest, that during the time men live without a common Power
> to keep them all in awe, they are in that condition which is called Warre;
> and such a warre, as is of every man, against every man.
>
> — Chapter XIII

War, he explains, is not only actual fighting but also the known disposition to it,
as foul weather is not one shower but rather a tendency to rain over many days. In
that condition nothing that depends on cooperation is possible: no industry,
agriculture, navigation, building, science, or art, "and which is worst of
all, continuall feare, and danger of violent death; And the life of man,
solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short."

### Was there ever such a time?

Hobbes anticipates the objection that no such condition ever existed. His
answer is threefold. First, it does not matter: the argument is about what
would follow from human nature without government, not about history. Second,
there is evidence anyway, in the "savage people" of America who live under no
government beyond the family, and in the way civilised people behave when
government collapses into civil war. Third, and most tellingly, sovereign
states are in this condition with respect to one another at all times, armed,
watchful, and at war in the extended sense.

He also points out that readers who doubt the picture already act on it: they
lock their doors, ride armed, and lock their chests even within their own
houses. That is what they think of their neighbours, their fellow citizens,
and their servants when the law is not looking.

### No justice, no property

In the natural condition nothing can be unjust, because justice and injustice
are creatures of law, and there is no law without a common power to make and
enforce it. Nor is there property: there is only what each person can take
and keep for as long as they can keep it. Right and wrong, mine and thine,
are qualities of people in society, not of people alone.

### The way out

The chapter ends by turning from description to remedy. The passions that
incline people to peace are fear of death, desire for the things that make
life comfortable, and hope of getting them by their own industry. Reason then
suggests the articles of peace on which people can agree, which Hobbes calls
the laws of nature and sets out in the next two chapters. The state of nature,
in other words, contains its own exit: the same self-interest that makes it a
war makes leaving it rational.
