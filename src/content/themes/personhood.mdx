The word "person" does an enormous amount of work in *Leviathan*. The
Introduction calls the commonwealth an *artificial man*; Chapter XVI
explains what it means for something to be a person at all; Chapter XVII
defines the commonwealth as a multitude "united in one Person"; and Part
III extends the same idea to God. Read together, these give a theory of
personhood that was new in 1651 and that has been argued about ever since.

### Persons are made, not found

Hobbes's definition is deliberately thin. A person is not a rational
substance, a soul, or a bearer of rights; it is whoever's words and actions
are being *considered* as belonging to someone, whether truly or by fiction.
This turns personhood from a fact about what a thing is into a fact about
how it is treated. A bridge can be a person, if someone is authorised to
speak for it. A living human being is a person in the natural sense because
their words are taken as their own.

The theatrical origin matters here. Hobbes reminds the reader that *persona*
meant a mask, and that to personate is to play a part. Personhood is a role,
and roles can be assigned. That is what makes it possible to build a state
out of it.

### The state as a person

The problem Part I leaves unsolved is that a multitude cannot act. It has as
many wills as members and no way to make them one. Hobbes's answer is not to
find a will they share but rather to give them a single representer, whose
acts they all own:

> For it is the Unity of the Representer, not the Unity of the Represented,
> that maketh the Person One.

The commonwealth is this artificial person: a multitude that has become one
by authorising one actor. The sovereign is not the state; the sovereign
*bears the person* of the state, wears its mask, and speaks with its voice.
This is a subtler picture than the one Hobbes is often credited with. The
state is not the sovereign's property, and it is not the people either. It is
a third thing, made out of the people by the act of representation, and it
persists as the sovereign who represents it changes.

Quentin Skinner has argued that this idea, the state as a *purely
artificial* person distinct from both rulers and ruled, is Hobbes's most
original contribution to political thought, and the origin of the modern
concept of the state as an impersonal entity. It is also why Hobbes can hold
that sovereignty may sit in one person or an assembly without the argument
changing: the person of the state is the same either way.

### Lesser persons

The same machinery explains everything below the sovereign. Chapter XXII
treats "systemes," the bodies within a commonwealth such as towns,
companies, colonies, and families, as lesser artificial persons, each with a
representative authorised by its members and all of them subordinate to the
person of the state. Modern corporate personhood, the idea that a company
can own, contract, sue, and be sued as a single legal person, descends from
this way of thinking, though the roots are older in Roman and canon law.

### God personated

The most controversial application comes in Part III. Since Hobbes has
allowed that "The true God may be Personated," he reads the Trinity as three
personations of the one God: by Moses, by Christ, and by the apostles and
their successors. Three persons in the Hobbesian sense, that is, three bearers
of God's words and actions, not three substances in one. The reading kept the
doctrine's vocabulary while replacing its metaphysics, and it drew immediate
charges of heresy. Hobbes softened it in the Latin edition of 1668, but the
underlying move, treating the Trinity as a case of his general theory of
representation, is characteristic. Nothing in the book is exempt from its
definitions.

### What personhood is not

It is worth noticing what Hobbes's account leaves out. It says nothing about
dignity, inherent worth, or the moral status of human beings as such. Those
are the concerns of later theories of personhood, Kantian and legal, that
often use Hobbes's vocabulary while rejecting his premises. For Hobbes,
personhood is a device for attributing actions, and its purpose is to make
obligation possible. That is why the chapter about it sits between the laws
of nature and the generation of the commonwealth: it is the hinge on which
the one turns into the other.
