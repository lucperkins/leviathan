If the commonwealth is the artificial man, sovereignty is what Hobbes calls
its soul: the thing that gives the whole body motion and without which it is
a corpse. The word names both the *power* the state holds and the *office*
that holds it, and the person or assembly in that office is the sovereign.

### Where it comes from

Sovereignty is created by the covenant that founds the commonwealth. Each
member of the multitude agrees with every other to authorise one person or
assembly to act for them all. The sovereign is the beneficiary of that
agreement, not a party to it, which has a consequence Hobbes presses hard:
the sovereign cannot break the covenant, because the sovereign never made
one. Subjects who claim the sovereign has breached faith with them are
pointing to an agreement that does not exist.

### What it includes

Chapter XVIII enumerates the rights of sovereignty and grounds them all in the
purpose of the institution:

> And because the End of this Institution, is the Peace and Defence of them
> all; and whosoever has right to the End, has right to the Means; it
> belongeth of Right, to whatsoever Man, or Assembly that hath the
> Soveraignty, to be Judge both of the meanes of Peace and Defence; and also
> of the hindrances, and disturbances of the same; and to do whatsoever he
> shall think necessary to be done, both beforehand, for the preserving of
> Peace and Security, by prevention of discord at home and Hostility from
> abroad; and, when Peace and Security are lost, for the recovery of the
> same.
>
> — Chapter XVIII

The specific rights follow: to make and repeal law, to judge all disputes, to
make war and peace and levy the money for it, to choose counsellors and
ministers, to reward and punish, to award titles of honour, and, the one that
most alarmed Hobbes's readers, to decide what may be taught:

> Sixtly, it is annexed to the Soveraignty, to be Judge of what Opinions and
> Doctrines are averse, and what conducing to Peace; and consequently, on
> what occasions, how farre, and what, men are to be trusted withall, in
> speaking to Multitudes of people; and who shall examine the Doctrines of
> all bookes before they be published.
>
> — Chapter XVIII

Hobbes's reasoning is that people act on their beliefs, so whoever controls
what is publicly believed controls whether there will be peace. A sovereign
without this right is a sovereign whose subjects can be preached into
rebellion.

### Absolute

Hobbes uses the word without embarrassment:

> So it appeareth plainly, to my understanding, both from Reason, and
> Scripture, that the Soveraign Power, whether placed in One Man, as in
> Monarchy, or in one Assembly of men, as in Popular, and Aristocraticall
> Common-wealths, is as great, as possibly men can be imagined to make it.
>
> — Chapter XX

"Absolute" here does not mean arbitrary or unlimited in fact; it means
*unconditioned by any higher human authority*. There is no court, estate, or
church that can overrule the sovereign, because if there were, that body
would be the sovereign instead. Hobbes anticipates the objection that this
is a miserable condition for subjects, and answers that any condition is
better than the war of all against all, and that the harms people fear from
absolute power are small next to the harms they have actually suffered from
its absence.

### Indivisible

The claim Hobbes cared about most, writing after a civil war fought over
exactly this, is that sovereignty cannot be shared out. A constitution that
gives the power of law to one body, the power of the purse to another, and
the power of the sword to a third has not moderated sovereignty but
rather destroyed it, because when the bodies disagree there is no one to decide,
and the dispute returns to force. The same goes for a separate spiritual
authority:

> where one is Soveraign, another Supreme; where one can make Lawes, and
> another make Canons; there must needs be two Common-wealths, of one & the
> same Subjects; which is a Kingdome divided in it selfe, and cannot stand.
>
> — Chapter XXIX

### The form does not matter, the unity does

Hobbes is usually taken for a defender of monarchy, and he does argue in
Chapter XIX that monarchy is the most convenient form. But the argument of
*Leviathan* does not depend on it. Sovereignty may sit in one person, in an
assembly of some, or in an assembly of all; what it may not do is sit in
several places at once. This is why Hobbes could be read as a royalist in
1651 and, a few years later, as a justification for obeying Cromwell.

### Where it ends

Sovereignty is absolute, but it is not eternal, and it is not everything.
It cannot command a subject to kill or maim themselves, or to refuse to
resist those who would, because the right of self-preservation was the
reason for submitting in the first place and cannot be signed away. And it
lasts exactly as long as it works:

> The Obligation of Subjects to the Soveraign is understood to last as long,
> and no longer, than the power lasteth, by which he is able to protect them.
>
> — Chapter XXI

A sovereign who can no longer protect has ceased, in Hobbes's terms, to be
one. Chapter XXI, which sets out when that happens, gives the theme its
image: "The Soveraignty is the Soule of the Common-wealth; which once
departed from the Body, the members doe no more receive their motion from
it." Chapter XXIX then describes, cause by cause, how commonwealths come to
that point.
