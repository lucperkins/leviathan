*Leviathan* was written in Paris between 1649 and 1651, by a man who had fled
England eleven years earlier, in the months after the King was executed and
while the outcome was still being settled by force. The war is the reason the
book exists. It is also, on the surface, barely present in it: the phrase
["the late Civill warre"](/chapters/the-epistle-dedicatory/) appears exactly
once, in the dedication, and "the late troubles in England" once more. There is
no narrative of the fighting, no mention of <Gloss note="The battle of 14 June
1645, near the Northamptonshire village. The New Model Army destroyed the
King's main field army in an afternoon and captured his private
correspondence, which was published to show he had been seeking Irish and
foreign help.">Naseby</Gloss> or of <Gloss note="The scaffold built against the
Banqueting House in Whitehall, where Charles I was beheaded on 30 January 1649.
Hobbes was in Paris, and was finishing Leviathan within months of it.">the
scaffold at Whitehall</Gloss>.

The single naming is personal rather than political. Hobbes dedicates the book
to Francis Godolphin, in memory of his brother Sidney, who was

> unfortunately slain in the beginning of the late Civill warre, in the
> Publique quarrel, by an indiscerned, and an undiscerning hand.
>
> — [The Epistle Dedicatory](/chapters/the-epistle-dedicatory/)

That is the whole of the war as event. Everything else is the war as argument.

### The diseases of a common-wealth

Chapter 29 is where the diagnosis lives, and its method is deliberately
clinical. Commonwealths do not die of old age; they die of doctrines, and
Hobbes lists them the way a physician lists symptoms:

> I observe the Diseases of a Common-wealth, that proceed from the poyson of
> seditious doctrines; whereof one is, "That every private man is Judge of
> Good and Evill actions."
>
> — Chapter 29

The catalogue that follows is the closest thing in the book to a history of
what went wrong. Private judgement of good and evil; the belief that acting
against conscience is always sin; the claim to supernatural inspiration; the
notion that the sovereign is subject to the civil laws; absolute property in
one's goods against the sovereign's right; the division of sovereign power;
imitation of neighbouring nations and of the Greeks and Romans; want of money;
monopolies; popular and ambitious men; and, last, the liberty of disputing
against sovereign power. Each of these was a live position in England in the
1640s, and Hobbes treats each not as a mistake in politics but rather as a
mistake in reasoning that had a body count.

### Mixed government, and the man with another man growing out of his side

The doctrine he handles most savagely is the one the King's own party had
adopted in 1642: that England was governed by three estates, King, Lords, and
Commons, each with a share of sovereignty. Hobbes regards this as a
contradiction dressed as a constitution, and reaches for anatomy to say so.
A commonwealth whose power of the purse, power of command, and power of
legislation lie in different hands is not a mixed government but rather three
factions sharing a name, and he can find no disease to compare it to:

> But I have seen a man, that had another man growing out of his side, with an
> head, armes, breast, and stomach, of his own: If he had had another man
> growing out of his other side, the comparison might then have been exact.
>
> — Chapter 29

### The ghostly authority

The deeper cause, for Hobbes, is the one Part III and Part IV are written to
remove. When a second authority claims to speak for God, subjects are given
two masters and the state is torn between them: canons set against laws, "a
Ghostly Authority against the Civill," working on men's minds with
distinctions that mean nothing. Presbyterian assemblies, Independent
congregations, and the papacy differ in every other respect and are, on this
point, the same disease.

### The schooling in rebellion

The third cause is a curriculum. In Chapter 21 Hobbes traces the English
appetite for tyrannicide to the reading of classical authors, who lived in
republics and wrote their local arrangements into books that schoolboys then
took for political science:

> by reading of these Greek, and Latine Authors, men from their childhood have
> gotten a habit (under a false shew of Liberty,) of favouring tumults, and of
> licentious controlling the actions of their Soveraigns.
>
> — Chapter 21

This is why Chapter 30 makes the instruction of the people a duty of the
sovereign, and why Part IV ends at the universities. If the war was caused by
opinions, the remedy is to control where opinions are formed.

### What he did about it

The Review and Conclusion turns the theory on its author. Having argued
throughout that protection and obedience are reciprocal, Hobbes draws the
conclusion his royalist friends in Paris found intolerable: a subject whose
sovereign can no longer protect him may submit to whoever can, without
injustice. He then acted on it, returning to England in the winter of 1651–52
and making his peace with the Council of State. The book that was read in
Paris as a defence of monarchy was read in London as a licence to submit to
the new regime, and both readings drew on the same argument.

Hobbes did eventually write a proper history of the war, *Behemoth*, in
dialogue form around 1668. Charles II refused it a licence, and it appeared
only after his death.
