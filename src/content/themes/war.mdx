Hobbes wrote *Leviathan* in Paris, in exile, while the wars of the three
kingdoms were ending in the execution of the king and the abolition of the
monarchy. War is the book's occasion and its constant reference point. But
Hobbes does something unusual with it: he defines it, and the definition
turns war from a thing that happens into a condition that obtains.

### War as a disposition

> For WARRE, consisteth not in Battell onely, or the act of fighting; but in
> a tract of time, wherein the Will to contend by Battell is sufficiently
> known: and therefore the notion of Time, is to be considered in the nature
> of Warre; as it is in the nature of Weather.

Foul weather is not a single shower but rather a tendency to rain over
days; war is not a single battle but rather a stretch of time in which
everyone knows that fighting may come. On this definition two neighbours
who distrust each other and keep their weapons ready are at war though no
blow has been struck, and a country in which the law is not enforced is at
war though it looks peaceful. Peace, correspondingly, is "all other time,"
the condition in which the disposition to fight is absent because something
holds it down.

This is why Hobbes can describe the natural condition as a war "of every
man, against every man" without claiming that people in it are always
fighting. They are always ready to, and that readiness is enough to make
industry, cultivation, building, and knowledge impossible, since none of
them is worth undertaking when the fruit may be taken by force.

### The morality of war

In the state of war there is no justice, because there is no law:

> Force, and Fraud, are in warre the two Cardinall vertues.

The sentence is often quoted as cynicism. It is closer to a definition.
Virtue, for Hobbes, is whatever conduces to one's end; where the end is
survival in a war, force and fraud are what conduce to it, and the
ordinary virtues of peace, keeping faith, dealing fairly, are not merely
absent but rather dangerous. The laws of nature that begin in Chapter XIV
are rules for getting out of this condition, and the first of them is to
seek peace as far as there is any hope of it, and otherwise to use all the
helps and advantages of war.

### The war between states

Hobbes concedes that there may never have been a time when individuals were
in a general state of war. But he points to one place where the condition
unmistakably exists:

> But though there had never been any time, wherein particular men were in
> a condition of warre one against another; yet in all times, Kings, and
> persons of Soveraigne authority, because of their Independency, are in
> continuall jealousies, and in the state and posture of Gladiators; having
> their weapons pointing, and their eyes fixed on one another; that is,
> their Forts, Garrisons, and Guns upon the Frontiers of their Kingdomes;
> and continuall Spyes upon their neighbours; which is a posture of War.

Sovereign states have no sovereign over them, so they stand to one another
as individuals do in the natural condition. This passage founded a tradition
in international thought that still bears Hobbes's name. It also contains
his quiet consolation: because states protect the industry of their
subjects, their perpetual war does not produce the misery that the war of
individuals would, and so it is tolerable in a way that anarchy among
persons is not.

### The sovereign's war

Within the commonwealth, war belongs to the sovereign alone. Chapter XVIII
lists it among the rights that cannot be separated from sovereignty:

> Ninthly, is annexed to the Soveraignty, the Right of making Warre, and
> Peace with other Nations, and Common-wealths; that is to say, of Judging
> when it is for the publique good, and how great forces are to be
> assembled, armed, and payd for that end; and to levy mony upon the
> Subjects, to defray the expenses thereof.

The dispute over exactly this right, whether the king or Parliament
commanded the militia, was the immediate cause of the English Civil War,
and Hobbes's readers would have heard the passage as a verdict on it. A
sovereign who cannot judge when to fight and raise the means to do so is
not a sovereign, and a state with two authorities over its armed force is
already in a condition of war with itself.

### The soldier and the innocent

Two limits follow from Hobbes's own premises. The first concerns the
soldier. Because no one can give up the right to defend their own life,
military service is not owed unconditionally:

> Upon this ground, a man that is commanded as a Souldier to fight against
> the enemy, though his Soveraign have Right enough to punish his refusall
> with death, may neverthelesse in many cases refuse, without Injustice; as
> when he substituteth a sufficient Souldier in his place: for in this case
> he deserteth not the service of the Common-wealth.

Hobbes adds that "there is allowance to be made for naturall timorousnesse,
not onely to women, (of whom no such dangerous duty is expected,) but also
to men of feminine courage." Running away is dishonourable, not unjust. The
exception closes when the commonwealth itself is at stake, or when a man has
enlisted and taken the sovereign's money.

The second concerns the enemy. Chapter XXVIII, on punishment, holds that
punishing an innocent subject is against the law of nature, but that harm
done to the innocent in war is a different matter, because enemies are
outside the covenant and the law of nature permits whatever the defence of
the commonwealth requires. Hobbes is not a theorist of just war in the
scholastic tradition. War for him is simply the natural condition returning
wherever the common power ends, and everything that is permitted in that
condition is permitted in war.
