Hobbes was sixty-three when *Leviathan* appeared in 1651. Galileo's
*Dialogue* was nineteen years old, Harvey's book on the circulation of the
blood twenty-three, Descartes's *Meditations* ten. The Royal Society would be
founded nine years later. Hobbes knew most of the people involved, and he
thought of his political philosophy not as a commentary on this movement but
rather as a part of it: the last and hardest science, built on the same foundations
as the others.

### What science is

Hobbes's definition, in Chapter V, is not about experiment or observation but
rather about a certain kind of knowledge:

> And whereas Sense and Memory are but knowledge of Fact, which is a thing
> past, and irrevocable; Science is the knowledge of Consequences, and
> dependance of one fact upon another: by which, out of that we can
> presently do, we know how to do something els when we will, or the like,
> another time; Because when we see how any thing comes about, upon what
> causes, and by what manner; when the like causes come into our power, wee
> see how to make it produce the like effects.

Science, on this view, is knowledge of how things are *generated*: what
causes produce what effects. Knowing that the sun rose yesterday is fact;
knowing why it rises is science, and the test of science is that it lets you
make things happen. This is a builder's conception of knowledge, and it
explains why Hobbes thought politics could be a science at all. The state is
a thing people make; a science of it would tell them how.

### Geometry as the model

The only existing science that met Hobbes's standard was geometry:

> And therefore in Geometry, (which is the onely Science that it hath pleased
> God hitherto to bestow on mankind,) men begin at settling the
> significations of their words; which settling of significations, they call
> Definitions; and place them in the beginning of their reckoning.

Geometry starts from definitions, proceeds by steps each of which is
undeniable, and reaches conclusions that no one can dispute. Hobbes came to
it late, at about forty, and the story Aubrey tells, of Hobbes reading
Euclid's forty-seventh proposition in a gentleman's library and exclaiming
that it was impossible, then working backward through the proofs until he
was convinced, is the story of a conversion. From then on his ambition was to
do for morals and politics what Euclid had done for figures. *Leviathan*'s
long opening chapters of definitions are the result.

### Everything is body in motion

The content of Hobbes's science is as important as its method. He is a
thoroughgoing materialist: the universe is body, and everything that happens
is motion. Sense is motion in the organs; imagination is that motion
decaying; the passions are small beginnings of motion inside the body;
reasoning is a kind of reckoning. There are no immaterial substances, no
forms, no purposes in nature. This is why he thought the philosophy taught in
the universities was not merely mistaken but rather not philosophy at all:

> The naturall Philosophy of those Schools, was rather a Dream than Science,
> and set forth in senselesse and insignificant Language; which cannot be
> avoided by those that will teach Philosophy, without having first attained
> great knowledge in Geometry: For Nature worketh by Motion; the Wayes, and
> Degrees whereof cannot be known, without the knowledge of the Proportions
> and Properties of Lines, and Figures.
>
> — Chapter XLVI

"Nature worketh by Motion" is the whole programme in four words. It is also
what Hobbes took from Galileo.

### The company he kept

Hobbes's scientific education was personal as much as bookish. In the 1620s
he worked for a time as Francis Bacon's secretary, taking dictation and
helping to put some of the essays into Latin, though he later thought little
of Bacon's inductive method. On his continental journeys with his aristocratic
pupils he sought out the new philosophy directly. In Paris in the 1630s he
joined the circle around the friar Marin Mersenne, the clearing-house of
European science, where he met Gassendi and corresponded with Descartes. In
1636 he visited Galileo in Florence, an encounter he remembered for the rest
of his life; in his own physics he would call Galileo the first to open the
door to natural philosophy.

He admired William Harvey, whose demonstration of the circulation of the
blood he saw as the model of a new doctrine established by reasoning and
proof, and the account of "vital motion" that opens Chapter VI is Harvey's
physiology turned into philosophy. With Descartes the relationship was
hostile. Hobbes wrote the third set of objections to the *Meditations* in
1641, denying that thinking implies an immaterial thinker, and the two men
never forgave each other. Each thought the other had stolen from him.

### Boyle and the Royal Society

The most famous quarrel came after *Leviathan*. When the Royal Society was
founded in 1660, Hobbes, the most notorious natural philosopher in England,
was not elected and never would be. In 1661 he published a *Dialogus
Physicus* attacking Robert Boyle's experiments with the air-pump and the
whole experimental method the Society stood for. Boyle answered at length,
and the exchange ran for years.

The dispute was about what counts as knowledge. Boyle and the Society held
that a "matter of fact" established by an experiment performed before
credible witnesses was the foundation of natural knowledge, and that
disputes about underlying causes could be set aside. Hobbes held that this
was precisely backwards: facts are not science, causes are, and a room full
of gentlemen watching a pump proved nothing about the nature of air or the
possibility of a vacuum. Worse, a body that claimed authority over knowledge
independent of the sovereign looked to him like every other private
authority that had torn England apart. Steven Shapin and Simon Schaffer's
*Leviathan and the Air-Pump* (1985) made this episode a landmark in the
history of science, and its argument is, in part, Hobbes's: that how a
society decides what is true is a political question.

Hobbes lost. Experimental science became science, and Hobbes's reputation as
a natural philosopher was buried by his long and hopeless attempt to square
the circle against the mathematician John Wallis. But the questions he put
to Boyle have not gone away, and the picture of nature in *Leviathan*, bodies
in motion and nothing else, is closer to what came after than most of what
the Royal Society's members believed.
