A reader who stops at the end of Part II will not guess how much of
*Leviathan* is about the Jews. Moses is named more often than any other person
in the book; Israel, Abraham, Aaron, and the high priests run through the whole
of Part III. The reason is structural rather than devotional. Hobbes has argued
that a common-wealth is instituted when a multitude covenants to authorise one
sovereign, and he needs a case in which that actually happened. Scripture, he
thinks, supplies exactly one.

### A kingdom by covenant

The argument turns on reading the kingdom of God literally rather than as a
metaphor for heaven. In Chapter XXXV, the phrase names a real polity with a
territory and a constitution:

> constituted by the Votes of the People of Israel in peculiar manner; wherein
> they chose God for their King by Covenant made with him, upon Gods promising
> them the possession of the land of Canaan.
>
> — Chapter XXXV

Everything Hobbes requires is in that sentence. There is a multitude, an act of
consent, a sovereign, and a consideration. The covenant at Sinai is a
common-wealth by institution, on the same pattern as the one derived from
reason in Chapter XVII, and it is the only one in history whose sovereign was
God himself.

### Abraham, Moses, and the high priests

Chapter XL works through the succession, and the marginal headings give the
argument away: Abraham had the sole power of ordering the religion of his own
people, and no subject of his could plead a private revelation against him.
The same holds for Moses, who was sovereign under God for his whole life
even though Aaron held the priesthood, and afterwards for the high priests.

The point being established is always the same one. At no stage were there two
authorities, a civil and a spiritual, standing side by side. Whoever held the
sovereignty also held the right to say what God had commanded, and the
priesthood was an office within the state rather than a rival to it.

### The demand for a king

Then the arrangement ends, and Hobbes makes as much of that as of its
founding. When the people ask Samuel for a king like the other nations, God's
answer is that it is not Samuel they have rejected:

> "they have rejected mee, that I should not reign over them." Having therefore
> rejected God, in whose Right the Priests governed, there was no authority
> left to the Priests, but such as the King was pleased to allow them.
>
> — Chapter XL

The peculiar kingdom is dissolved by the same means that made it, the consent
of the people, and the priestly authority does not survive it on its own
account. After the captivity, Hobbes adds, the Jews had no settled
common-wealth at all.

### What the example is for

The whole reconstruction is aimed at seventeenth-century Rome and at
seventeenth-century Westminster. If the one commonwealth God ever ruled
directly kept its religious and civil power in a single pair of hands, then a
church claiming jurisdiction over a Christian prince is claiming something
without precedent, and the argument against
[Bellarmine](/interlocutors/bellarmine/) in Chapter XLII leans on Israelite
history at nearly every step. The Urim and Thummim were given to a high priest
who was also the civil sovereign; the seventy elders of Numbers held their
authority from Moses, who held the sovereignty. Each example is turned to the
same conclusion.

### What is not here

It is worth being clear about the limits of the subject. Hobbes writes about
the ancient Israelites as a constitutional case study, drawn entirely from the
Hebrew Bible as Christians received it. He has essentially nothing to say
about Judaism as a living tradition: the Talmud and the rabbis are absent from
the book, and so are the Jewish communities of his own century, including the
question of their readmission to England, which was being argued in London in
the very years he was writing.

His framing is also the supersessionist one standard among Protestant writers
of the period. The kingdom by covenant is treated as a dispensation that
lapsed and is to be restored by Christ at the resurrection, and the Jews
appear as the former subjects of that kingdom rather than as contemporaries.
Read as political theory the chapters are careful and original; read as an
account of Judaism they are not really an account of Judaism at all.
