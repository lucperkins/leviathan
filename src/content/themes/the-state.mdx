The book's title names its subject. Hobbes borrows the sea monster of the Book
of Job to stand for the state, and he tells the reader on the first page what
kind of thing he takes the state to be:

> For by Art is created that great LEVIATHAN called a COMMON-WEALTH, or STATE,
> (in latine CIVITAS) which is but an Artificiall Man; though of greater
> stature and strength than the Naturall, for whose protection and defence it
> was intended.
>
> — Introduction

The state is *made*. It is not, as Aristotle taught, a natural growth, nor a
divine institution handed down through kings. It is an artefact, built by
people out of themselves, for a purpose. Everything in *Leviathan* follows
from taking that idea seriously.

### Why the argument starts with sense

A modern reader picks up a political treatise and finds the first six
chapters are about perception, imagination, language, and reasoning. This is
not a digression. Hobbes's method is to build from the smallest parts: if the
state is an artificial man, you must first understand the natural man it is
built from. Part I ("Of Man") works upward from sensation to the passions,
from the passions to the condition people would be in without government, and
from there to the laws of nature that show the way out. Only in Part II ("Of
Common-wealth") does the state itself appear.

### What the state is for

The purpose is stated repeatedly and simply: peace and defence. Without a
common power, people who are roughly equal in strength and cunning, who want
the same scarce things, and who cannot trust one another, fall into a war of
all against all. The state exists to end that condition and keep it ended.
Everything else it does, such as making law, judging disputes, raising armies,
and regulating doctrine, is instrumental to that one end.

This gives Hobbes an unusual position. He is not interested in the *good*
state in the classical sense, the one that makes citizens virtuous. He is
interested in the *effective* state, the one that actually secures the
conditions under which people can live. A state that cannot protect its
subjects has, for Hobbes, lost the only claim it ever had on their obedience.

### What the state must be

From the purpose, Hobbes derives the form. Chapter XVIII lists the rights of
the sovereign: to be free from any covenant with the subjects, to be
unaccountable to them, to judge what doctrines may be taught, to make law, to
decide disputes, to make war and peace, to choose ministers, to reward and
punish, and to bestow honour. Chapter XIX argues that while these rights can
be held by one person, a few, or an assembly of all, they cannot be *divided*
between different bodies. A "mixed" constitution, in which king, lords, and
commons each hold part of the sovereignty, is not a moderate state but rather a
recipe for civil war, because when the parts disagree there is no one to
decide.

Hobbes was writing during and just after the English Civil War, and the
argument against divided sovereignty is his diagnosis of what had gone wrong.

### What the state may not do, and what it cannot

Hobbes is often read as licensing total power, and the sovereign's rights are
certainly extensive. But two limits follow from his own premises. First, the
subject's right of self-preservation cannot be given up, because the whole
point of submission was to preserve oneself; no one is bound to kill,
wound, or refuse to defend themselves at the sovereign's command. Second, the
obligation to obey lasts exactly as long as the sovereign's ability to
protect. Chapter XXI ends with the observation that when a sovereign can no
longer defend the subjects, "The Obligation of Subjects to the Soveraign is
understood to last as long, and no longer, than the power lasteth, by which
he is able to protect them."

### Dissolution

Part II closes with a chapter "Of those things that Weaken, or tend to the
Dissolution of a Common-wealth." Hobbes's list reads like a commentary on
1640s England: sovereigns who accept limits on their power, the doctrine that
private conscience is the measure of good and evil, the idea that the
sovereign is subject to the civil laws, the division of power, the influence
of popular classical authors who praise republics and call kings tyrants, and
above all the claim that spiritual power is independent of civil power. The
state, for Hobbes, is a fragile artefact that people are constantly tempted to
take apart.
