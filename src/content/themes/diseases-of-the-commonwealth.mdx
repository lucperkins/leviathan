The [artificial man](/themes/artifice/) of the Introduction is given an
anatomy — sovereignty for a soul, magistrates for joints, wealth for strength
— and the list ends, without a change of tone, in pathology: concord is its
health, sedition its sickness, and civil war its death. Chapter 29 is what
that sentence looks like worked out at length.

### A physician's list

The chapter is organised as a diagnosis, and Hobbes keeps the medical
vocabulary all the way through rather than dropping it after the first
paragraph. A sovereign who lacks the power he needs has an infirmity the
commonwealth was born with, like a child got by an ill midwife. Where
subjects claim a private judgment of good and evil, or a private inspiration,
the resulting fits of obedience and disobedience are compared to the falling
sickness:

> And this is a Disease which not unfitly may be compared to the Epilepsie, or
> Falling-sicknesse (which the Jewes took to be one kind of possession by
> Spirits) in the Body Naturall.

Money hoarded by monopolists is a pleurisy, the treasure gathering where it
should be flowing. A treasury emptied faster than it fills is an ague, the
veins not supplied from the arteries. Corporations inside a state are
described, memorably, as "many lesser Common-wealths in the bowels of a
greater, like wormes in the entrayles of a naturall man". And the list of
lesser complaints at the end is almost a joke, except that Hobbes does not
seem to be joking:

> We may further adde, the insatiable appetite, or Bulimia, of enlarging
> Dominion; with the incurable Wounds thereby many times received from the
> enemy; And the Wens, of ununited conquests, which are many times a burthen,
> and with lesse danger lost, than kept; As also the Lethargy of Ease, and
> Consumption of Riot and Vain Expence.

### Why it is not decoration

A metaphor can be a way of avoiding an argument. This one is a way of making
it. If the commonwealth is a made body, then its failures are mechanical
failures with causes, and a cause can be traced, which is exactly the claim
Hobbes needs against the view that a state falls because God is displeased or
because the times are corrupt. Naming a disease commits you to a cause and a
cure, and Chapter 29 supplies both: every disease on the list turns out to be
a doctrine, and the cure is teaching the right one, which is what
[Chapter 30](/chapters/30-of-the-office-of-the-soveraign-representative/) is
for.

It also fixes the limits of his pessimism. A body that can be sick is not a
body that must die of any particular illness. Hobbes says as much, and the
sentence is unusually hopeful for him: though nothing mortals make can be
immortal, if men had the use of the reason they pretend to, their
commonwealths might be secured at least from perishing by internal diseases.
Set beside the [artificial eternity](/themes/artifice/) of succession in
Chapter 19, the position is that a state need not die of anything but
conquest.

### The doctrines themselves

Stripped of the medicine, the list is a catalogue of opinions, and it is the
one place where the abstract argument touches the war Hobbes had just watched.
That every private man may judge good and evil; that whatever a man does
against his conscience is sin; that faith comes by supernatural inspiration;
that the sovereign is subject to the civil laws; that subjects have absolute
property against him; that sovereignty may be divided; that a neighbouring
nation's constitution is worth imitating; and that the Greek and Latin
authors are a fit education for gentlemen. Each is answered somewhere earlier
in the book, and each was held by somebody who had recently taken up arms.
The historical half of that is the subject of
[the English Civil War](/themes/the-english-civil-war/), and Hobbes wrote the
long version twenty years later in [Behemoth](/works/behemoth/).
