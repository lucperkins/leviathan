Chapter XII, "Of Religion," sits in the middle of Part I between the
chapters on manners and on the natural condition of mankind. Its placement
is the argument: before Hobbes can show why people need a sovereign, he has
to account for the one thing that most reliably sets them against one. He
does it by explaining religion as he has explained everything else in Part
I, from the inside of a human being outward.

### A definition among the passions

Religion first appears in the catalogue of passions in Chapter VI, defined
in a single line alongside its shadow:

> Religion Superstition; True Religion-- Feare of power invisible, feigned
> by the mind, or imagined from tales publiquely allowed, RELIGION; not
> allowed, superstition.

> And when the power imagined is truly such as we imagine, TRUE RELIGION.

The definition is deliberately provocative. Religion and superstition are
the same passion, fear of invisible power; the only difference is whether
the tales it rests on are publicly allowed. Which of the two a belief is
depends on the law, not on the belief. True religion is a third case, where
the power feared really is as it is imagined, but Hobbes gives no test for
telling it from the other two except revelation.

### The natural seed

Chapter XII asks where the passion comes from and answers that it is
peculiar to human beings:

> Seeing there are no signes, nor fruit of Religion, but in Man onely; there
> is no cause to doubt, but that the seed of Religion, is also onely in Man;
> and consisteth in some peculiar quality, or at least in some eminent
> degree thereof, not to be found in other Living creatures.

That quality is curiosity about causes. People want to know why things
happen, and especially why good and bad things happen to them; where they
cannot find a cause they suppose one; and what they suppose is a power like
themselves but invisible. Chapter XI had already named the feeling:

> And this Feare of things invisible, is the naturall Seed of that, which
> every one in himself calleth Religion; and in them that worship, or feare
> that Power otherwise than they do, Superstition.

Chapter XII gathers the ingredients into a list:

> And in these foure things, Opinion of Ghosts, Ignorance of second causes,
> Devotion towards what men fear, and Taking of things Casuall for
> Prognostiques, consisteth the Naturall seed of Religion; which by reason
> of the different Fancies, Judgements, and Passions of severall men, hath
> grown up into ceremonies so different, that those which are used by one
> man, are for the most part ridiculous to another.

Ghosts, because people cannot conceive of a spirit except as a thin body;
ignorance of second causes, because they do not know the mechanism behind
events; devotion to what they fear, because they treat invisible powers the
way they treat powerful men; and omens, because they mistake coincidence for
sign. None of these is a mistake about God. They are ordinary features of
how human beings, as Part I describes them, think.

### Cultivated by two sorts of men

The seed does not grow by itself:

> For these seeds have received culture from two sorts of men. One sort have
> been they, that have nourished, and ordered them, according to their own
> invention.

> Of the former sort, were all the Founders of Common-wealths, and the
> Law-givers of the Gentiles: Of the later sort, were Abraham, Moses, and
> our Blessed Saviour; by whom have been derived unto us the Lawes of the
> Kingdome of God.

The pagan founders, Hobbes says, shaped religion on purpose to make people
obedient, peaceable, and willing to blame their own failings rather than
their rulers, and he describes the Roman policy of tolerating every cult in
the city as a masterpiece of the kind. The second sort acted by God's
command. But the account of how religion functions in a commonwealth is the
same for both. Religion is the most powerful instrument of civil order there
is, and the most dangerous when it is in the wrong hands.

### Why religions change

The chapter ends with a theory of religious change, and it is not about
truth:

> For seeing all formed Religion, is founded at first, upon the faith which
> a multitude hath in some one person, whom they believe not only to be a
> wise man, and to labour to procure their happiness, but also to be a holy
> man, to whom God himselfe vouchsafeth to declare his will supernaturally;
> It followeth necessarily, when they that have the Goverment of Religion,
> shall come to have either the wisedome of those men, their sincerity, or
> their love suspected; or that they shall be unable to shew any probable
> token of divine Revelation; that the Religion which they desire to uphold,
> must be suspected likewise; and (without the feare of the Civill Sword)
> contradicted and rejected.

A religion stands or falls with the credit of its ministers. When they
demand belief in contradictions, when they live contrary to what they
preach, when their own interest is visible in their doctrines, faith fails,
and Hobbes traces every great change of religion in history to one cause:
"unpleasing Priests," Catholic and Reformed alike. The last line of the
argument, "without the feare of the Civill Sword," is the point. Religion
will not hold itself up; only the sovereign can.

### Natural religion and natural worship

Chapter XXXI, which closes Part II, draws the consequence for the state.
There is a kingdom of God by nature, over everyone who acknowledges his
providence, and its laws are the laws of nature. Worship in that kingdom is
of two kinds: natural signs of honour that reason discovers, and arbitrary
signs that men institute. Since a commonwealth is one person, its public
worship must be uniform, and it belongs to the sovereign to decide what
that uniformity is. Private opinion is left alone; public expression is
not. This is the bridge to Part III, where Hobbes asks what changes when
the sovereign, and the subjects, are Christians. The answer, worked out at
length, is: less than the churches claimed.
