Hobbes liked to say that [fear and he were born twins](/hobbes/life/): his mother, hearing
that the Spanish Armada was in the Channel, went into labour early, and he
arrived in April 1588 already frightened. The joke was also a thesis. No
philosopher before him had made fear so central, and few since have been
willing to say so plainly that a good political order is one that arranges
people's fears correctly.

### What fear is

In the catalogue of passions in Chapter VI, fear gets one line:

> Feare-- Aversion, with opinion of Hurt from the object, FEARE.

Fear is aversion plus a belief: an endeavour away from something, joined to
the opinion that the thing will hurt. That makes it a rational passion in
Hobbes's scheme, unlike terror or panic. It responds to what one thinks is
true, and it can be corrected by better information or redirected by
changing what there is to be afraid of. Everything Hobbes does with fear
depends on this.

### The fear that makes the state

The natural condition is defined by one fear above all others. Without a
common power "to keep them all in awe," people are at war, and the worst
thing about the war is "continuall feare, and danger of violent death." But
the same passion points the way out:

> The Passions that encline men to Peace, are Feare of Death; Desire of
> such things as are necessary to commodious living; and a Hope by their
> Industry to obtain them.
>
> — Chapter XIII

The fear that makes people dangerous to one another is the fear that makes
them willing to submit. Hobbes does not ask anyone to become virtuous or to
love their neighbour; he asks them to be afraid of the right thing. The
commonwealth is a machine for replacing the fear of everyone with the fear
of one.

### Fear and obligation

Because fear is a reason, and not an excuse, agreements made from fear are
binding:

> Covenants entred into by fear, in the condition of meer Nature, are
> obligatory.

Hobbes's example is a ransom: a prisoner who promises to pay for their life
is bound to pay. This was a deliberate break with the lawyers and moralists
who held that a promise extracted by fear was void. For Hobbes, every
promise worth having is extracted by fear of something, and the founding
covenant of the state is the largest example. The point is drawn out in
Chapter XX, where a commonwealth acquired by conquest, in which the subjects
submit out of fear of the conqueror, has exactly the same rights as one
instituted by agreement, in which they submit out of fear of each other:

> But in this case, they subject themselves, to him they are afraid of.

### Fear and liberty

Chapter XXI adds the last piece. Actions done from fear are still free,
because fear does not remove the power to do otherwise; it changes what one
wants to do. Hobbes's illustration is homely:

> It is therefore the action, of one that was Free; so a man sometimes pays
> his debt, only for Feare of Imprisonment, which because no body hindred him
> from detaining, was the action of a man at Liberty.

A subject who obeys the law for fear of punishment is not a slave; they are a
free person choosing, on good information, the safer course. Law works, in
Hobbes's account, precisely by attaching to each forbidden act a fear
greater than the desire for it.

### The fear that makes religion

Fear has a darker cousin. At the close of Chapter XI, on the threshold of
the chapter on religion, Hobbes traces religion itself to the fear of things
unseen:

> And this Feare of things invisible, is the naturall Seed of that, which
> every one in himself calleth Religion; and in them that worship, or feare
> that Power otherwise than they do, Superstition.

This fear is no less natural than the fear of death, but it is far less
reliable, because it attaches to causes that cannot be checked. Whoever can
shape what people fear in the invisible world can command them against their
sovereign, and Parts III and IV of the book are Hobbes's long campaign to
bring that fear under the same authority as every other.

### An honest politics

Hobbes's readers found this bleak, and many still do. But the alternative
he saw was not a politics without fear; it was a politics that pretended not
to run on it, and so left people's fears to be played on by priests,
demagogues, and rival lords. His answer was to make the sovereign the one
thing everyone fears more than they fear each other, and then to insist
that this is what peace is. It is not a comforting doctrine. It is, he would
say, a true one.
