Most political treatises are named for their subject; this one is named for
a monster. The name appears on the title page, once in the Introduction —

> For by Art is created that great LEVIATHAN called a COMMON-WEALTH, or
> STATE, (in latine CIVITAS) which is but an Artificiall Man
>
> — [The Introduction](/chapters/the-introduction/)

— once in [Chapter 17](/chapters/17-of-the-causes-generation-and-definition-of-a-common-wealth/),
where the covenant generates "that great LEVIATHAN, or rather (to speake
more reverently) of that Mortall God, to which wee owe under the Immortall
God, our peace and defence," and once more in
[Chapter 28](/chapters/28-of-punishments-and-rewards/), where Hobbes finally
stops to say why. Three appearances, in a book of half a million words, were
enough to rename the state in most of Europe's languages. They were also
enough to hand his enemies a weapon, because Hobbes had chosen, on purpose,
a creature with a past.

### The monster in Job

Leviathan enters the Bible as the untamable thing. At the climax of Job, God
answers the sufferer out of the whirlwind not with a justification but
rather with a zoology — an inventory of creation ending in two beasts
beyond human power, Behemoth on land and Leviathan in the sea. The
interrogation is a taunt
([Job 41:1](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_%28King_James%29/Job#41:1)):
"Canst thou draw out leviathan with an hook? or
his tongue with a cord which thou lettest down?" The chapter builds through
armored scales and breathed fire to the verses Hobbes wanted, which the
King James Version renders:

> Upon earth there is not his like, who is made without fear. He beholdeth
> all high things: he is a king over all the children of pride.
>
> — [Job 41:33–34](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_%28King_James%29/Job#41:33)

The [frontispiece](/frontispiece/) sets the first of these over the giant's
head in Latin, citing it by the Vulgate's numbering — *Non est potestas
Super Terram quae Comparetur ei. Iob. 41. 24* — and the Latin sharpens the
point, since the Vulgate's word for "his like" is *potestas*: no power upon
earth to compare with him. Chapter 28 then gives the verses in Hobbes's own
translation, and the paragraph is his entire official account of the title:

> Hitherto I have set forth the nature of Man, (whose Pride and other
> Passions have compelled him to submit himselfe to Government;) together
> with the great power of his Governour, whom I compared to Leviathan,
> taking that comparison out of the two last verses of the one and fortieth
> of Job; where God having set forth the great power of Leviathan, called
> him King of the Proud.
>
> — [Chapter 28](/chapters/28-of-punishments-and-rewards/)

The logic is in the epithet. Pride — each man's insistence on his own
judgment, his own worth, his own first place — is the passion that makes
men ungovernable, and the commonwealth is the one thing proud creatures
cannot outface: king over all the children of pride, made (as Hobbes
renders it) "so as not be afraid." The monster is not a lapse of taste but
rather a load-bearing part of the argument, which runs on
[fear](/themes/fear/): only an image of unanswerable power says what the
state must be in order to work. And Hobbes marks the analogy's limit in the
same breath — Leviathan is mortal, subject to decay, and there is that in
heaven he should stand in fear of. Job's monster fears nothing; Hobbes's
fears God and [dissolution](/themes/diseases-of-the-commonwealth/), which
is why the next chapter is the diseases. When Job's own question — why the
innocent suffer — comes up in [Chapter 31](/chapters/31-of-the-kingdome-of-god-by-nature/),
Hobbes answers it the same way God does: by right of irresistible power.

### The Devil's biography

Elsewhere in scripture Leviathan is something worse than untamable.
[Psalm 74](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_%28King_James%29/Psalms#74:14)
has God breaking the monster's heads in the founding combat;
[Psalm 104](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_%28King_James%29/Psalms#104:26)
shrinks him to a pet — "there is that leviathan, whom thou hast made to
play therein" — and
[Isaiah 27:1](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Bible_%28King_James%29/Isaiah#27:1)
promises his execution: "In that day the LORD
with his sore and great and strong sword shall punish leviathan the
piercing serpent... and he shall slay the dragon that is in the sea." Out
of these verses the Christian centuries built a biography. Gregory the
Great's commentary on Job made Leviathan the Devil himself, and medieval
art took the identification literally: the mouth of hell, gaping at the
edge of a thousand Last Judgments to swallow the damned, is Leviathan's
mouth, furnished with teeth from Job's chapter. The rabbis were friendlier —
in the Talmud the righteous will eat Leviathan's flesh at the banquet at
the end of days — but a seventeenth-century English reader met the name
knowing, roughly, that it belonged to the dragon God defeats.

Naming the state after *that* was a provocation, and Hobbes — who named his
history of the civil war [*Behemoth*](/works/behemoth/), completing the
pair, rebellion as the land monster to the state's sea monster — can hardly
have missed it. What he wanted from the name was its terror; what came with
the terror was its diabolism, and his readers proved unable, or unwilling,
to keep the two apart.

### What the analogy carries

The name is one half of a double image, and the halves pull against each
other. The Leviathan is a monster, the most fearsome of God's creatures;
the commonwealth is an [artificial man](/themes/artifice/), the most
intricate of ours — a made person, assembled by covenant out of the very
men it overawes, whose anatomy of soul, joints, and nerves has
[a page of its own](/themes/artifice/). The frontispiece holds both at
once: a crowned colossus, *composed* of the people, over the Vulgate's
verse. Chapter 17's phrase does the same compression in three words —
"Mortall God" — power that is worshipful, and made, and dies. The tension
is the theory. A state fearsome enough to end the war of all against all,
yet nothing over the men who authorised it but their own
[personated](/concepts/person/) strength: every reader who has felt the
book waver between reassurance and menace is feeling the two halves of the
name grind against each other.

### The hunting of Leviathan

The name did half his enemies' work for them. The refutations came titled
as monster-hunts, straight out of Job 41:1 — Alexander Ross's *Leviathan
Drawn out with a Hook* (1653), Bishop Bramhall's *The Catching of
Leviathan, or the Great Whale* (1658) — and their author became the Monster
of Malmesbury, the beast of his own book. The sport has never really
closed. Carl Schmitt argued in 1938 that the image was a genuine myth that
escaped its handler — chosen for its terror, remembered for its diabolism,
and eventually gutted by readers who made the great fish into a machine;
modern scholarship's standard account of the seventeenth-century uproar
takes its title from the chase, *The Hunting of Leviathan*. And yet by the
long measure the name won everything: in the political vocabulary of every
European language, *leviathan* now simply means the state — used most
fluently by people attacking it, which would not have surprised him.

### The poets' Leviathan

The monster kept its own life in poetry, usually nearer Job than Hobbes.
Milton — Hobbes's contemporary and opposite in nearly everything — moors
*Paradise Lost*'s pilot to the beast in a simile aimed at Satan's deceiving
bulk:

> or that Sea-beast
> Leviathan, which God of all his works
> Created hugest that swim th' Ocean stream:
> Him haply slumbring on the Norway foam
> The Pilot of some small night-founder'd Skiff,
> Deeming some Island, oft, as Sea-men tell,
> With fixed Anchor in his skaly rind
> Moors by his side under the Lee, while Night
> Invests the Sea, and wished Morn delayes
>
> — *Paradise Lost*, Book I (1667)

A thing so vast it is mistaken for solid ground, until it moves: it is hard
to read the lines, published sixteen years after *Leviathan*, without
hearing a comment on the neighbouring monster — the state men anchor their
lives to, deeming it an island. Two centuries later Melville built a whole
book inside the whale's name, and among the epigraphs of *Moby-Dick*'s
"Extracts," between scripture and the whalemen, sits the Introduction's
opening sentence, credited to Hobbes: the artificial man swimming in the
company of the real monsters at last. Painters kept faith with the older
story, in which the beast does not rule but rather dies.

That may be the last word on the analogy. In every tradition Hobbes
borrowed from, Leviathan is the power that gets hooked, caught, shown its
place, or slain; Hobbes alone drew him crowned. Whether the borrowing was
a master stroke or — as Schmitt thought — a self-inflicted wound, the
choice did what the book says names do: it fixed the thing in the mind. No
one has thought about the state without the monster since.
