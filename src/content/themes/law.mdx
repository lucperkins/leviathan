Hobbes complained that the writers before him used *law* and *right* as if
they were interchangeable, and he treats the confusion as more than careless.
The distinction he insists on is the one on which the argument for absolute
sovereignty turns:

> RIGHT, consisteth in liberty to do, or to forbeare; Whereas LAW,
> determineth, and bindeth to one of them: so that Law, and Right, differ as
> much, as Obligation, and Liberty; which in one and the same matter are
> inconsistent.
>
> — Chapter XIV

A right is a permission and creates no duty in anyone else; a law is a bond
and takes liberty away. It follows that a sovereign who is bound by the civil
law is not sovereign, and that a subject's liberty is whatever the law has
left unregulated rather than a set of claims held against the sovereign.

### The laws of nature

The first laws Hobbes names are not commands of any lawgiver. A law of nature
is a precept found out by reason forbidding a man to destroy his own life or
to omit the means of preserving it, and Chapters XIV and XV derive nineteen of
them, beginning with the rule to seek peace and the rule to lay down one's
right to all things so far as others will do the same. They are theorems about
self-preservation, and Hobbes says plainly that they oblige *in foro interno*,
in a desire that they take place, but not always *in foro externo*, in action,
because a man who keeps them when nobody else does merely makes himself prey.

That is the gap the commonwealth exists to close. Justice, the third law, is
the performance of covenants, and covenants are empty until something makes
breaking them the worse option:

> where there is no Common-wealth, there nothing is Unjust. So that the nature
> of Justice, consisteth in keeping of valid Covenants: but the Validity of
> Covenants begins not but with the Constitution of a Civill Power, sufficient
> to compell men to keep them.
>
> — Chapter XV

### Civil law

Chapter XXVI gives the definition the rest of Part II has been building
toward, and the emphasis falls on *who is speaking* rather than on what is
said:

> Law in generall, is not Counsell, but Command; nor a Command of any man to
> any man; but only of him, whose Command is addressed to one formerly
> obliged to obey him.
>
> — Chapter XXVI

Counsel is offered for the hearer's own benefit and may be refused; command is
addressed by someone whose will already binds. From this Hobbes draws the
consequences that made the chapter notorious. The sovereign alone is
legislator, and is not subject to the civil law, since whoever can repeal a
law at will is not bound by it. Custom has force only because the sovereign
tolerates it. And the laws of nature become civil laws once a commonwealth
exists, so that there are not two competing legal orders but rather one, in
which reason's precepts are enforced as the sovereign's commands.

The chapter also contains the sharpest of Hobbes's disagreements with an
English lawyer. Against [Sir Edward Coke's](/chapters/26-of-civill-lawes/)
doctrine that law is an artificial perfection of reason acquired by long
study, Hobbes answers that it is not wisdom but rather authority that makes a
law: were it wisdom, the study of many contradictory judges would produce
contradictory law, and there would be no way to settle which was binding.

### What law makes possible

Once law is in place the remaining political vocabulary follows quickly. A
sin is any transgression of a law or contempt of the legislator; a crime is
the narrower thing, a sin committed in deed or word against what the law
forbids or commands, so that intending to steal is a sin but not yet a crime.
Punishment is then defined in Chapter XXVIII as an evil inflicted by public
authority for a judged transgression, to the end that men's wills may be
better disposed to obedience.

The definition is stricter than it looks, and Hobbes uses it to rule things
out. Harm inflicted without public authority is not punishment but rather
hostility; harm for an act committed before the law existed is not punishment,
since there was no transgression; and harm exceeding what the law declared is,
beyond that point, not punishment either. The same move that makes the
sovereign's power absolute also makes it formal: the sovereign may command
what it likes, but what it does outside the announced law is not law's work
at all.
