Almost every philosopher before Hobbes treated reason as the highest part of
the soul, a power that apprehends things as they truly are. Hobbes defines it
as arithmetic:

> Reason, in this sense, is nothing but Reckoning (that is, Adding and
> Substracting) of the Consequences of generall names agreed upon, for the
> Marking and Signifying of our thoughts.
>
> — Chapter V

The deflation is deliberate and it is total. Reasoning is a kind of
computation, and it is performed not on things but rather on *names*. This is
why [speech](/chapters/04-of-speech/) has to be defined first: without names
there is nothing to reckon with, and Hobbes is explicit that creatures without
language cannot reason, however prudent they may be.

### What it operates on

Names are marks we impose to recall our own thoughts and signs by which we
show them to others. They are not natural, and they carry no authority in
themselves. From this Hobbes draws a consequence that governs the rest of the
book:

> Truth consisteth in the right ordering of names in our affirmations.
>
> — Chapter IV

Truth is a property of propositions, not of things, and a proposition is true
when its names are ordered according to the definitions we have agreed on.
There is no truth before definition. So the person who wants certainty must
begin by settling his terms, and the person who wants to deceive can do it
most effectively by leaving them unsettled.

### Reason and prudence

Reason is not experience. Hobbes separates them sharply, and the distinction
runs the length of Part I. Prudence is a presumption of the future built from
the memory of like cases, and animals have it. It is often reliable and never
certain, because the future is, as Hobbes puts it in Chapter III, a fiction of
the mind applying the sequels of past actions to present ones.
Reason is different in kind: it does not guess at what will follow but rather
computes what does follow, given the names. Science is the record of those
computations:

> Science is the knowledge of Consequences, and dependance of one fact upon
> another.
>
> — Chapter V

The practical difference is that experience cannot be transmitted whole,
while science can. A prudent man's advantage dies with him; a demonstrated
consequence can be set down and used by anyone who accepts the definitions.

### Why reason fails

Because reason is reckoning, it fails the way arithmetic fails: by starting
from the wrong figures, or by losing track of the sum. Hobbes's diagnosis of
philosophical absurdity is that philosophers begin in the middle:

> For there is not one of them that begins his ratiocination from the
> Definitions, or Explications of the names they are to use.
>
> — Chapter V

He quotes [Cicero](/interlocutors/cicero/) with evident pleasure to the effect
that nothing is so absurd that some philosopher has not said it, and adds that
Cicero was one of them. The full indictment waits until Chapter XLVI, where
philosophy itself is defined as knowledge acquired by reasoning from the
manner of a thing's generation to its properties, or back again, and the
schools are convicted of failing that test. Their abstract essences and
incorporeal substances are not false doctrines so much as strings of words
that were never given a meaning.

### The political consequence

If truth depends on definitions, and definitions rest on agreement, then a
common-wealth divided about its terms is divided about everything. This is
Hobbes's answer to the objection that men can reason their way to justice
without a sovereign. In a dispute, each party appeals to *right reason*, and
each means his own:

> And therefore, as when there is a controversy in an account, the parties
> must by their own accord, set up for right Reason, the Reason of some
> Arbitrator, or Judge, to whose sentence they will both stand, or their
> controversie must either come to blowes, or be undecided.
>
> — Chapter V

Reason therefore does not remove the need for a sovereign but rather
establishes it. The laws of nature are themselves called precepts "found out
by Reason" in Chapter XIV, and reason genuinely delivers them; what it cannot
deliver is agreement about
their application, which is why the same chapter moves immediately from
reasoning to covenanting.
