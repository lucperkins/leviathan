Hobbes never uses the phrase "social contract." What he describes in
Chapter XVII is a *covenant* of every person with every other. But the
structure is the one that has carried the name ever since: people in a
condition without government agree among themselves to create one, and the
authority of the government rests on that agreement. *Leviathan* is the
first fully worked-out version of the idea, and much of what came after is
either a revision of it or a reaction against it.

### What was new

The notion that rulers hold power by some kind of agreement was old. Medieval
and Reformation writers spoke of a contract between king and people, and
used it to argue that a king who broke his side could be resisted. Hobbes
took the idea and rebuilt it so that it could not be used that way. His
covenant is not between sovereign and subjects but rather among the subjects
themselves; the sovereign, not being a party, cannot breach it. The
agreement creates authority rather than limiting it.

The other novelty is the starting point. Hobbes does not begin from a people
already living under law and ask what they agreed to. He begins from
individuals with no obligations at all, each with a right to everything, and
asks what such individuals would rationally do. The answer, that they would
give up their right to all things in exchange for security, is derived from
their fear of death and their desire for commodious living, not from any
prior sense of justice. Justice is a *product* of the agreement, not its
basis.

### The argument's shape

Three moves, each in its own chapter. Chapter XIII shows that without a
common power, people are at war. Chapter XIV derives from that the laws of
nature, which are rules of reason for getting out of the war, and defines
contract and covenant as the means. Chapter XVII shows that the laws of
nature cannot work without a power to enforce covenants, and that the way to
create such a power is a covenant of all with all authorising a sovereign.
The reasoning is deliberately hypothetical. Hobbes does not claim any
existing state was founded this way; he claims that this is the reasoning
that makes any state's authority intelligible, and that a subject who
understands it will see why obedience is in their interest.

### Locke, Rousseau, Kant

The tradition divided almost immediately. John Locke's *Second Treatise*
(1689) kept the contract but changed the state of nature: a condition of
inconvenience rather than war, governed by a natural law that already
includes rights to life, liberty, and property. Government is instituted to
protect those pre-existing rights, is limited by them, and may be resisted
when it violates them. Locke's contract does what Hobbes designed his to
prevent.

Jean-Jacques Rousseau's *Social Contract* (1762) borrowed Hobbes's radical
starting point, individuals with no prior political ties, but made the
outcome a people governing itself through a general will, in which the
citizen obeys only laws they have given themselves. Immanuel Kant then
detached the contract from history and psychology altogether: it is an idea
of reason, a test of whether a law *could* have been agreed to by everyone,
not an account of how states arise or of what self-interested people would
do.

### Two branches

Modern political philosophy usually distinguishes the two lines that emerge.
*Contractarianism* in the strict sense is Hobbesian: it derives norms from
the agreement rational, self-interested agents would make, and it needs no
moral premises to start from. David Gauthier's *Morals by Agreement* (1986)
is the fullest recent statement, and James Buchanan's constitutional
economics belongs to the same family. *Contractualism*, by contrast, is
Kantian: it asks what principles could be justified to everyone as free and
equal persons, and it presupposes that persons have a standing that must be
respected. John Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* (1971), with its original
position behind a veil of ignorance, and T. M. Scanlon's account of what we
owe to each other, are the leading examples. Both use the idea of agreement;
only the first uses it the way Hobbes did.

### The standing objections

Two objections have followed the theory from the beginning. The first is
that no one actually agreed. Hume put it plainly in the eighteenth century:
almost every government was founded in conquest or usurpation, and the
consent of the governed is a fiction. Hobbes had partly anticipated this by
giving conquest, "common-wealth by acquisition," the same rights as
institution, and by making the argument hypothetical. Whether a hypothetical
agreement can bind anyone is the question that divides his successors.

The second is that a contract cannot bind before there is a power to
enforce it, which is exactly Hobbes's own principle. If covenants of mutual
trust are void in the state of nature, how can the founding covenant be
valid? Hobbes's answer is that this covenant is unlike the others because it
creates its own enforcer at the moment it is made. Whether that answer works
has been debated for three and a half centuries, and the debate is, in a
sense, what contractarianism is.
