The instrument that had governed the English press was the same court that
had cropped Prynne's ears: Star Chamber, and with it the licensing system
the bishops administered. Both went down in the summer of 1641, and the
press discovered there was no law left to break. The bookseller
George Thomason, who set out to buy everything, is the measure of what followed:
his shop took in a few hundred titles a year at the decade's start and
thousands a year by 1642 — sermons, petitions, remonstrances, libels,
prophecies, battle news true and invented. Parliament tried to rebuild the
dam with a Licensing Order in 1643, earning Milton's *Areopagitica* and its
"as good almost kill a Man as kill a good Book"; the order changed little.
For the first time anywhere, a whole political nation — down to apprentices
and, notoriously, women petitioners — conducted its revolution in cheap
print, week by week.

War made the press periodical. The court's *Mercurius Aulicus*, printed at
Oxford and smuggled into London, traded weekly volleys with *Mercurius
Britanicus*, written by <Gloss note="Marchamont Nedham (1620–1678), the
century's great turncoat journalist: Britanicus for Parliament, Pragmaticus
for the King, Politicus for the Commonwealth — and, on the engagement
question, a borrower from Hobbes. He reappears on the next-but-one
page.">Marchamont Nedham</Gloss> — the invention, more or less, of the
political weekly, and of the discovery that a war needs its narrative
managed on both sides.

### The Levellers

Out of London's print and the Army's ranks came the first movement in
Europe organised by pamphlet. The men called Levellers — John Lilburne,
whom no prison could silence, Richard Overton, William Walwyn — argued from
native rights and natural equality to conclusions no English government
would grant for centuries: a franchise for most men, religious toleration,
equality before the law, a written constitution. That constitution, *An
Agreement of the People*, was debated with Cromwell and Ireton at
the Putney debates in the autumn of 1647, where Colonel Rainborough gave
the movement its sentence: "For really I think that the poorest he that is
in England hath a life to live, as the greatest he."

What makes the Levellers uncanny reading beside *Leviathan* is how much of
the toolkit is shared. Overton's *An Arrow Against All Tyrants* grounds
everything in self-propriety — "to every individual in nature is given an
individual property by nature, not to be invaded or usurped by any" — and
builds government from consent, delegation, and the ends it must serve.
Natural equality, natural right, authority as a trust from the people:
these are Hobbes's own premises, aimed the other way. Where
[Chapter 13](/chapters/13-of-the-naturall-condition-of-mankind/) reads
equality as the reason men must surrender judgment to a sovereign, the
Levellers read it as the reason no sovereign may take it; the shared
arsenal is why Hobbes thought such doctrines more dangerous than the
royalists' — they were his own artillery, captured. The Army's grandees
felt the danger from the other side, and settled the argument without
paper: the Leveller mutinies were broken at Burford church in May 1649,
three weeks after the King's execution had made the Agreement briefly
imaginable.

### The Diggers

The Levellers hated the name they were given, and in April 1649 a handful
of poor men on <Gloss note="A hillside common at Walton-on-Thames in
Surrey, where Gerrard Winstanley's company began digging and planting on 1
April 1649. Harassed by suits, beatings, and the pulling down of their
houses, they moved to nearby Cobham heath and were finally dispersed in the
spring of 1650.">St George's Hill</Gloss> showed why, by meaning it
literally. Gerrard Winstanley's True Levellers — the Diggers — took the
commons with spades, declaring in *The True Levellers Standard Advanced*
that the earth was made "a common treasury" for all, that buying and
selling the land was the Fall itself, and that the young Commonwealth,
having beheaded kingly power, should now abolish its root in property.
Winstanley wrote that the command had come to him in a trance: "Worke
together; Eat bread together." A man commanded by revelation to dissolve
property is, from *Leviathan*'s side of the argument, two of
[Chapter 29](/chapters/29-of-those-things-that-weaken-or-tend-to-the-dissolution-of-a-common-wealth/)'s
diseases walking in one body — pretended
[inspiration](/concepts/prophet/), and the doctrine that subjects hold
property against the sovereign, inverted into holding it against everyone.
And yet on the bare theory of property the two men agree against Locke
before Locke: for Hobbes too there is no mine and thine in nature, and
property is a creature of the commonwealth — which is exactly why, in his
view, no private conscience gets to redesign it.

The sects the collapse of licensing released — Baptists, Seekers, Ranters,
soon Quakers, every congregation printing and every man potentially his own
[prophet](/concepts/prophet/) — are told on the
[religion in England](/context/religion-in-england/) page; the press was
their element as much as the Levellers'. And the century's greatest
pamphlet belonged to the other side: *Eikon Basilike* (*The Royal
Portrait*), the King's book of meditations, appearing within days of the scaffold and running through
dozens of editions in a year, manufactured the royal martyr faster than
Milton's official *Eikonoklastes* (*The Image-Breaker*) could unmake him. Print, which had
disarmed the monarchy, armed its ghost.

### Hobbes's diagnosis

Hobbes drew one lesson from the whole flood: the war had been a war of
opinions before it was a war of armies, and the pen was not a metaphor for
the pike but rather its cause. That is why
[Chapter 18](/chapters/18-of-the-rights-of-soveraignes-by-institution/)
places the government of doctrine at the centre of sovereignty itself, in
words that read like a licensing act deduced from first principles:

> For the Actions of men proceed from their Opinions; and in the wel
> governing of Opinions, consisteth the well governing of mens Actions, in
> order to their Peace, and Concord.
>
> — [Chapter 18](/chapters/18-of-the-rights-of-soveraignes-by-institution/)

The sovereign is to judge what doctrines are fit to be taught, and who
"shall examine the Doctrines of all bookes before they be published."
Upstream of the presses Hobbes located the reservoir: the universities,
which trained the preachers and gentlemen who wrote the pamphlets — in
*Behemoth* he calls them the core of the rebellion — and so the remedy runs
through the curriculum. The [Review and Conclusion](/chapters/a-review-and-conclusion/)
ends with the author's modest proposal that his book "may be profitably
printed, and more profitably taught in the Universities."

The irony was not lost on Hobbes's enemies, and should not be lost on us:
*Leviathan* is itself an artifact of the freedom it would abolish — written
in English rather than Latin, sold by Andrew Crooke at the Green Dragon in
St Paul's Churchyard among the sermons and the newsbooks, one more argument
on a bookstall that the arguing should end. The war that all this paper first talked into
being is [the next page](/context/the-english-civil-war/).
