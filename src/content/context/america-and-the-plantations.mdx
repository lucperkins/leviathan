The state of nature is an inference, not a history, and Hobbes is careful to
say so. Take away the sovereign in thought, reason from the passions men
demonstrably have, and the war of every man against every man follows —
whether or not it ever happened. But having proved the theorem, he glances
up from the page, anticipates the objection, and points, just once, at the
actual world:

> It may peradventure be thought, there was never such a time, nor condition
> of warre as this; and I believe it was never generally so, over all the
> world: but there are many places, where they live so now. For the savage
> people in many places of America, except the government of small Families,
> the concord whereof dependeth on naturall lust, have no government at all;
> and live at this day in that brutish manner, as I said before.
>
> — [Chapter 13](/chapters/13-of-the-naturall-condition-of-mankind/)

It is the only empirical evidence the argument is ever offered, and it is
worth reading slowly. The hedges are doing real work — *many* places, not
all; *except* the government of small families, which is to say not no
government after all — and the claim is not that Americans are men in some
earlier or lesser state of humanity but rather that men anywhere, without a
common power, live like this. Still, the sentence enrols America in the
argument: the famous inventory of everything the natural condition lacks —
"no Culture of the Earth; no Navigation... no Arts; no Letters; no Society"
— is also, unmistakably, a colonist's inventory of what a plantation was
for.

### The company

Hobbes's America was not only read; part of it passed across his desk. His
employer <Gloss note="William Cavendish (1590–1628), from 1626 the second
Earl of Devonshire: Hobbes's pupil on the first continental tour and then
his master and close friend. His early death in 1628 turned Hobbes out of
the household for a time, and the Thucydides translation is dedicated to his
memory.">William Cavendish</Gloss> was a substantial adventurer in the
Virginia Company, and from 1622 to 1624 Hobbes attended
thirty-seven of its recorded meetings, holding a share of his own. These were the company's
death-throes: two factions — Sandys's against Smythe's and Warwick's —
fighting for control in packed and shouting courts, pamphleteering against
each other, appealing to the Crown, until in 1624 James I dissolved the
company altogether and took Virginia for the first crown colony. A young
philosopher's closest view of a self-governing assembly was of one tearing
itself apart.

Those same meetings dealt with the aftermath of the event that hardened
English colonial thinking: the attack of March 1622, in which
Opechancanough's warriors killed some three hundred and forty-seven
colonists in a morning. The company's own printed response drew the lesson
that treaties with the natives were dissolved and their lands now forfeit
to a juster war. Whether or not Hobbes ever endorsed that argument, he
plainly knew it; it is the exact shape of the clause he would drop, almost
casually, into his chapter on colonies thirty years later.

### The procreation of commonwealths

[Chapter 24](/chapters/24-of-the-nutrition-and-procreation-of-a-common-wealth/)
treats economics under a biological figure — nutrition is commodities,
concoction is money — and when it reaches reproduction, the children of a
commonwealth turn out to be its colonies:

> The Procreation, or Children of a Common-wealth, are those we call
> Plantations, or Colonies; which are numbers of men sent out from the
> Common-wealth, under a Conductor, or Governour, to inhabit a Forraign
> Country, either formerly voyd of Inhabitants, or made voyd then, by warre.
>
> — [Chapter 24](/chapters/24-of-the-nutrition-and-procreation-of-a-common-wealth/)

"Made voyd then, by warre" is five words long and carries the whole violence
of the century in it, stated without justification or regret, as a fact
about how commonwealths breed. The same chapter derives property itself from
the sovereign — in the state of nature nothing is mine or thine — so the
question that vexed Spanish theologians and English promoters alike, of
what right dispossession could rest on, has for Hobbes a short answer:
right, like property, begins where the commonwealth does.

[Chapter 30](/chapters/30-of-the-office-of-the-soveraign-representative/)
returns to colonies as policy, and produces the strangest passage of the
three. The sovereign's duty to the poor runs through transplantation, the
transplanted are told to be intensive farmers rather than ranging
dispossessors — and then the argument runs off the edge of the inhabited
world:

> The multitude of poor, and yet strong people still encreasing, they are to
> be transplanted into Countries not sufficiently inhabited: where
> neverthelesse, they are not to exterminate those they find there; but
> constrain them to inhabit closer together, and not range a great deal of
> ground, to snatch what they find; but to court each little Plot with art
> and labour, to give them their sustenance in due season. And when all the
> world is overchargd with Inhabitants, then the last remedy of all is
> Warre; which provideth for every man, by Victory, or Death.
>
> — [Chapter 30](/chapters/30-of-the-office-of-the-soveraign-representative/)

Not exterminate, but rather constrain: the moderation and the violence are
the same sentence, and the chapter ends the colonial question where the
book began, in the war of all against all — this time not behind the
commonwealth but rather ahead of it, waiting at the world's carrying
capacity.

### What was made of it

The Chapter 13 sentence had a long afterlife. Locke sharpened the thought
Hobbes had hedged — "in the beginning all the World was *America*" — and
attached it to a theory of property that made unfenced land waste waiting
for an owner, which Hobbes's theory, where property waits on sovereignty
rather than labour, notably does not. Later centuries read the "savage
people" passage as the founding gesture of a European habit: writing real
nations into the prehistory of the state, as raw material for a thought
experiment. That case, and the replies to it, belong to the
[post-colonial reading](/readings/post-colonial/); what the historical
record adds is only that Hobbes wrote the passage as neither a traveller
nor an innocent, but rather as a man who had sat in the meetings where a
plantation was argued over, share by share, massacre to dissolution. By the
time he wrote it, England's arguments about kings, churches, and property
had found a marketplace of their own — the London press, which is
[the next page](/context/the-pamphlet-war/).
