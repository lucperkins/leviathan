England's Reformation was an act of state before it was a movement of the
spirit. The Act of Supremacy of 1534 made the King "the only supreme head in
earth of the Church of England," and the church's doctrine, worship, and
lands were thereafter settled by statute, changed by statute, and changed
back — Protestant under Edward, Roman under Mary, Protestant again under
Elizabeth. Hobbes regarded the supremacy not as an innovation but rather as
the recovery of a right that every sovereign holds by nature, and Chapter 42
is several hundred pages of argument that this is so. But the supremacy
answered only the question of who governs the church. What the church should
be — its ceremonies, its bishops, its relation to the men in the pews — was
answered by no one for a century, and the arguing parties eventually became
armies.

### The half-reformed church

The Elizabethan settlement of 1559 restored a church that was Protestant in
doctrine, episcopal in government, and traditional in dress: a compromise
built to comprehend as many consciences as possible. The people who could
not be comprehended acquired a nickname, Puritans, and a conviction that the
reformation had stopped halfway — a church, they said, but halfly reformed.
The radical wing wanted the whole apparatus of bishops replaced by the
Presbyterian discipline: government by assemblies of elders, holding their
authority *jure divino*, directly from God. To Hobbes this was the papal
claim relocated rather than abolished — a spiritual power over subjects that
the civil sovereign does not grant and cannot recall. The position he holds
instead, that church government belongs wholly to the state, had been argued
in the previous century by [Thomas Erastus](/kindred/thomas-erastus/), whose
name became its label.

James I, who had been raised under Scotland's presbytery and loathed it,
saw the stakes at once. At the Hampton Court conference in 1604 he dismissed
the Puritan petitioners with the aphorism Hobbes's whole politics endorses:
"No bishop, no King." A discipline that chooses its own governors, he meant,
will not stop at the church.

### Laud, and the beauty of holiness

Under Charles I the pendulum swung the other way. William Laud, Archbishop
of Canterbury from 1633, set about enforcing what he called the beauty of
holiness: altars railed at the east end, ceremonies observed, lecturers
suppressed, conformity exacted through the church courts, High Commission,
and Star Chamber. In 1637 the lawyer William Prynne and two fellow
pamphleteers had their ears cropped on the pillory for writing against the
bishops — gentlemen punished like felons, which shocked more people than
the pamphlets had persuaded. To the Puritans the whole programme, with a
Catholic queen at court, looked like popery returning by the church door;
thousands emigrated to Massachusetts rather than conform. And the Laudian
bishops made a claim that quietly damaged their own foundation: that their
office was *jure divino*, held from God rather than from the King — the
detail Hobbes fastens on in Chapter 47, since a bishop by divine right is,
in miniature, the same "ghostly authority" problem as a pope.

### Scotland pulls the thread

What broke the structure was the attempt to extend it. In 1637 Charles and
Laud imposed a new prayer book on Presbyterian Scotland without consulting
its parliament or its assembly; the first reading in St Giles' in Edinburgh
ended in a riot. The Scots swore the National Covenant in 1638, abolished
their bishops, and raised an army; the two Bishops' Wars that followed
bankrupted the King, and a bankrupt king must summon a parliament. The Long
Parliament met in November 1640 and turned at once on the church: Laud was
in the Tower within months, the Root and Branch petition demanded episcopacy
be destroyed "with all its dependencies, roots and branches," and the
censorship collapsed, releasing a flood of sects — Independents,
Baptists, and soon stranger things, every congregation its own judge of
doctrine and every man potentially his own [prophet](/concepts/prophet/).
Hobbes had already left: he fled to Paris that same November, by his own
account the first of all that fled. [The war itself](/context/the-english-civil-war/)
has a page of its own, further down this shelf.

### The knots untied

Hobbes tells this whole story once, near the end of the book, and his
version is a small masterpiece of controlled irony. Chapter 47 describes the
growth of clerical power as three knots tied on Christian liberty — first
the authority of assemblies, then bishops, then the pope — and observes
that England untied them in reverse order:

> First, the Power of the Popes was dissolved totally by Queen Elizabeth;
> and the Bishops, who before exercised their Functions in Right of the
> Pope, did afterwards exercise the same in Right of the Queen and her
> Successours; though by retaining the phrase of Jure Divino, they were
> thought to demand it by immediate Right from God: And so was untyed the
> first knot.
>
> — Chapter 47

Then the Presbyterians "obtained the putting down of Episcopacy," untying
the second; then power was taken from the Presbyterians in their turn, and
the last knot came loose:

> And so we are reduced to the Independency of the Primitive Christians to
> follow Paul, or Cephas, or Apollos, every man as he liketh best: Which, if
> it be without contention, and without measuring the Doctrine of Christ, by
> our affection to the Person of his Minister, (the fault which the Apostle
> reprehended in the Corinthians,) is perhaps the best.
>
> — Chapter 47

"Perhaps the best" is a startling sentence from the great theorist of the
state church, and it was written in 1651, under an Independent-dominated
regime whose protection Hobbes was about to accept. It is less a conversion
than a consistency: what he opposed was never this or that form of worship
but rather any spiritual power the sovereign does not hold — and a church
dissolved into congregations, "without contention," claims none. Whether
Independency without contention was on offer in 1651 is a question the
[civil war](/context/the-english-civil-war/) had already answered.
