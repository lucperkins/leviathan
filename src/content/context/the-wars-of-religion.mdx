Hobbes was born in 1588, [in the week the Armada was expected](/hobbes/life/),
and published *Leviathan* in 1651, three years after the Peace of Westphalia.
His whole life fits inside Europe's wars of religion, and the book assumes a
reader for whom that history needed no rehearsing. That religion causes war
was not, for Hobbes's first readers, a provocation; it was the plainest fact
of the age. What *Leviathan* adds is a diagnosis of why, and a cure that most
of them found worse than the disease.

### One church into many

When Luther published his theses in 1517, the quarrel looked like a dispute
among clergy. Within a generation it had broken the fiction that Latin
Christendom was one body under one spiritual head, and had handed every
prince in Europe a new and dangerous question: whose doctrine runs in my
lands? The Peace of Augsburg in 1555 answered it with the formula
<Gloss note="'Whose the rule, his the religion': the tag later coined to
summarise the Peace of Augsburg, by which each prince of the Empire chose
Lutheranism or Catholicism for his own territory, and subjects who would not
conform were free to emigrate.">*cuius regio, eius religio*</Gloss> — the
prince's religion is the territory's religion. It is worth noticing what this
was: a peace made on Hobbes's principle a full century before Hobbes, by
exhausted men who had discovered that doctrine could not be settled by
argument and would otherwise be settled by pikes. It kept the Empire quiet
for sixty years, and then failed.

### France

France ran the experiment that England would later repeat, and Hobbes watched
England with France in his memory. Between 1562 and 1598 the kingdom fought
eight civil wars over religion, punctuated by the Saint Bartholomew's Day
massacre of 1572, in which Paris killed some thousands of its Protestant
neighbours in a week. Out of the pamphlet war came the
<Gloss note="'King-fighters': the resistance theorists of the late sixteenth
century — Huguenot pamphlets such as the Vindiciae contra tyrannos (1579)
after the massacre, and Jesuit writers such as Juan de Mariana (De rege,
1599) — who argued that a heretical or tyrannical king might lawfully be
resisted, deposed, or killed.">monarchomachs</Gloss>, who supplied learned
arguments that a king of the wrong religion might be resisted or killed, and
each confession took its turn using them. A Catholic zealot murdered Henri
III in 1589; another murdered Henri IV in 1610 — the year the
twenty-two-year-old Hobbes first crossed to the continent as a tutor, into a
France still talking about the knife.

France also produced the answer Hobbes would radicalise. The *politiques*
concluded that civil peace must outrank doctrinal truth, and
[Jean Bodin](/kindred/jean-bodin/), writing four years after the massacre,
gave sovereignty its modern definition: a power absolute, perpetual, and
above all indivisible. *Leviathan*'s debt to that conclusion is everywhere,
and the difference is only that Hobbes thought Bodin had not gone far enough:
a sovereignty that stops at the church door has not ended the war but rather
adjourned it.

### Germany

The Thirty Years' War began in 1618, when Protestant nobles threw two royal
governors out of a window in Prague, and ended thirty years and perhaps a
third of Germany's population later. It was never only a war of religion —
dynasties and France's rivalry with the Habsburgs kept it burning — but
religion started it, recruited for it, and made it unnegotiable for a
generation; the sack of Magdeburg in 1631 became the century's byword for
what confessional war does to a city. It was the largest fact in Europe for
the whole of Hobbes's middle life. While the diplomats finished the Peace of
Westphalia, Hobbes sat in Paris [writing *Leviathan*](/hobbes/life/); the
peace they produced generalised Augsburg — religion follows the sovereign,
now with the Calvinists included — and buried the idea of a Christendom with
a single enforceable faith.

### The theorem

*Leviathan* turns this history into an argument, and the argument has two
steps. The first is an anatomy of religion itself: Chapter 12 derives it from
ignorance of causes and fear of invisible powers, which is why it can be
cultivated, and why it changes. When Hobbes surveys why religions lose their
grip, his examples are the Reformation's:

> So that I may attribute all the changes of Religion in the world, to one
> and the some cause; and that is, unpleasing Priests; and those not onely
> amongst Catholiques, but even in that Church that hath presumed most of
> Reformation.
>
> — Chapter 12

The second step is the diagnosis of the wars. They happen, Hobbes argues,
wherever two authorities claim the same subjects — wherever a
[church](/concepts/church/) exists that is not simply the commonwealth
worshipping. His formulation in Chapter 39 is the whole century in one
sentence:

> Temporall and Spirituall Government, are but two words brought into the
> world, to make men see double, and mistake their Lawfull Soveraign.
>
> — Chapter 39

This is what the [frontispiece](/frontispiece/) draws: the sword and the
crozier in the two hands of one figure. It is also why half of *Leviathan* is
about religion. Part III argues that a Christian sovereign is the only
lawful interpreter of scripture in his dominions, with
[Cardinal Bellarmine](/touchstones/bellarmine/), the papacy's greatest
controversialist, given a book-length refutation in Chapter 42; Part IV, on
the [Kingdome of Darknesse](/concepts/kingdom-of-darkness/), is a history of
how the clergy came to hold a power Hobbes considers usurped. The European
wars are the proof text. England, which fought its own version while he
wrote, is [the next page](/context/religion-in-england/).
