The regicide left England's new masters with an old problem in its sharpest
form: nearly everyone who mattered had sworn something to the King —
allegiance, the Protestation, the Solemn League and Covenant — and the
government that had killed him needed their obedience anyway. Its
instrument was a promise of studied minimalism, the Engagement: "I do
declare and promise, that I will be true and faithful to the Commonwealth
of England, as it is now established, without a King or House of Lords."
Required of councillors and officeholders in 1649, it was extended in
January 1650 to every man in England over eighteen. The point was not
sincerity but rather subscription; and the question of whether a man
forsworn to a dead king could honestly subscribe set off three years of
pamphleteering that historians call the Engagement controversy — the
context into which *Leviathan*, in the spring of 1651, actually landed.

### The de facto men

The Commonwealth's defenders rarely argued that the new regime was
legitimate in the old way; they argued that legitimacy of the old sort was
the wrong test. Francis Rous, whose *The Lawfulnes of Obeying the Present
Government* opened the debate within months of the scaffold, reasoned from
Romans 13 and providence: the powers that be are ordained of God, and God
had visibly changed the powers. Anthony Ascham reasoned from protection:
government exists so that men may live, whoever holds it; when possession
changes, obedience follows, or nothing does. And
<Gloss note="Marchamont Nedham (1620–1678), lately the royalist Mercurius
Pragmaticus, arrested in 1649 and converted to the Commonwealth's service;
from June 1650 he edited its official weekly, Mercurius Politicus. His
career is the engagement question lived rather than argued.">Marchamont
Nedham</Gloss>, in *The Case of the Common-wealth of England, Stated*
(1650), put the whole position with a journalist's bluntness — the sword
creates title, and the people's interest is peace under whoever wields it —
before bolstering a later edition with quotations from a philosopher the
reader had possibly not heard of: Thomas Hobbes, whose
[*Elements of Law*](/works/elements-of-law/) had said the necessary things
about protection and obedience years before the war ended.

### What Leviathan says

Hobbes had not written his book as an Engagement pamphlet; its argument was
[twenty years old](/works/elements-of-law/) and its printing was underway
before the controversy peaked. But its last pages meet the moment so
exactly that no reader in 1651 could miss it. The doctrine was already
there in [Chapter 21](/chapters/21-of-the-liberty-of-subjects/), stated as
a theorem:

> The Obligation of Subjects to the Soveraign is understood to last as
> long, and no longer, than the power lasteth, by which he is able to
> protect them.
>
> — [Chapter 21](/chapters/21-of-the-liberty-of-subjects/)

The [Review and Conclusion](/chapters/a-review-and-conclusion/) makes the
application explicit, answering the controversy's precise casuistical
question — not *whether* to submit but rather *when* submission becomes
lawful:

> the point of time, wherein a man becomes subject of a Conquerour, is that
> point, wherein having liberty to submit to him, he consenteth, either by
> expresse words, or by other sufficient sign, to be his Subject.
>
> — [A Review and Conclusion](/chapters/a-review-and-conclusion/)

A man still in arms, or maintained by the old sovereign's garrisons, has no
such liberty; a man whose king can no longer feed or shield him has
discharged all he was obliged to, and may seek protection where it exists —
even a soldier, once the field is lost. Conquest itself is redefined to
fit: not victory, which is a fact about armies, but rather the *submission*
of the vanquished, which is a covenant. Even at the last, the
Commonwealth's title would come not from its sword but rather from each
Englishman's consent to stop running — the same consent, by
[Chapter 20](/chapters/20-of-dominion-paternall-and-despoticall/), that
institution and acquisition have always shared. And when Hobbes states the
book's purpose in its final paragraphs, the sentence could stand as the
motto of the whole controversy: it was written "without other designe, than
to set before mens eyes the mutuall Relation between Protection and
Obedience."

### Paris reads it first

The people best placed to take offence were the ones Hobbes lived among.
He had a fair manuscript copy on vellum presented to the young
<Gloss note="Charles Stuart, nineteen when Leviathan appeared, was at Paris
between defeats: crowned by the Scots at Scone in January 1651, routed at
Worcester in September, and back in France, a king entirely of the de jure
sort, by October. The presentation copy survives in the British
Library.">Charles II</Gloss>, his sometime pupil in mathematics; within
weeks he was forbidden the court. The book's enemies there were partly
clerical — Anglican divines enraged by its ecclesiology, French Catholics
by its anti-popery — but the Review and Conclusion supplied the charge that
stuck: the tutor had written the beaten side a permission slip for
desertion. Clarendon, who was there, later recorded the plainest version of
Hobbes's defence, from the man himself: "The truth is, I have a mind to go
home." He went — crossing in the winter of 1651–52, submitting to the
Council of State, and living out the Interregnum unmolested in London,
precisely as his last chapter said a man might.

### The long argument about what he meant

The taint never faded. John Wallis jeered after the Restoration that
*Leviathan* had been written in defence of Oliver's title; Hobbes replied
that when he wrote it Oliver had no title to defend, and that the book
fights on behalf of all sovereigns, under whatever name they hold their
power — which was, for once in a polemic, exactly true. Filmer had said the
same thing [as an accusation](/context/divine-right-and-absolutism/) in
1652: a sovereignty built on consent and protection travels to whoever can
protect, and cannot mourn. The modern recovery of the Engagement debate —
the discovery that *Leviathan*'s first public read it beside Ascham and
Nedham rather than beside Locke — is one of the founding results of the
[contextualist reading](/readings/contextualist/), and it cuts both ways:
the book is more partisan than the philosophers assumed, and less than the
royalists charged. Hobbes's own last word on the period is
[*Behemoth*](/works/behemoth/)'s, and it is not about titles at all: the
war ended, he wrote there in effect, as *Leviathan* said all wars end — not
when a cause triumphed, but rather when a people, having gone the full
circle from sovereignty to anarchy and back, consented at last to be
protected. He had, after all, a mind to go home.
