*Leviathan* is an absolutist book in an age of absolutist theories, and its
first readers measured it against the ones they knew. Sovereignty absolute
and undivided; the king above the law he makes; no right of resistance —
every one of these conclusions was already on the shelf. What was new, and
to the theory's older friends unforgivable, was the foundation. The
absolutisms of Hobbes's century derived the sovereign's right from above,
from God or from Adam; Hobbes derives it from below, from the fear and
[consent](/concepts/contract/) of the men to be ruled. The conclusions
match. The premises are at war.

### Bodin's discovery

The theory begins in France, in the wreckage of the
[wars of religion](/context/the-wars-of-religion/).
[Jean Bodin](/kindred/jean-bodin/), writing his *Six livres de la
République* (*Six Books of the Commonwealth*) in 1576, four years after the
Saint Bartholomew's massacre,
defined sovereignty as the absolute and perpetual power of a commonwealth,
and added the clause that mattered most: it cannot be divided. A state where
one body makes law and another judges it, or where a king shares power with
estates, has not distributed sovereignty but rather destroyed it. Hobbes
took the doctrine whole — [Chapter 18](/chapters/18-of-the-rights-of-soveraignes-by-institution/)
is Bodin's indivisibility argued from contract — and then removed Bodin's
furniture. Bodin's sovereign remained bound by divine and natural law, by
the fundamental laws of the realm, and by his subjects' property, which he
could not tax without consent. To Hobbes each of these reservations is a
door left open, and a sovereignty with doors in it is the
[war](/context/the-english-civil-war/) arriving on schedule.

### Kings as gods

England's version was theological. The divine right of kings, as James I
expounded it — in *The Trew Law of Free Monarchies* (1598), and to his
parliaments for years afterward — held that kings hold their crowns
immediately from God, are accountable to him alone, and may be disobeyed by
no subject on any pretext, though a tyrant will answer for it at a higher
bar. "Kings are justly called Gods," he told Parliament in 1610, "for that
they exercise a manner or resemblance of Divine power upon earth." The
theory is easy to mock and was not silly: it was built as a wall against
two live threats — the papal claim to depose heretic princes, which
[Bellarmine](/touchstones/bellarmine/) defended and *Leviathan* answers at
book length, and the <Gloss note="The Calvinist and Jesuit resistance
theorists of the previous century, discussed on the wars of religion page,
who had supplied learned arguments for deposing or killing a king of the
wrong religion.">monarchomach</Gloss> case for resistance. Divine right and
*Leviathan* are answers to the same two enemies, which is why they agree
about obedience; but a right held from God needs no subjects' consent, and
that is the whole quarrel.

The patriarchal variant, argued by Sir Robert Filmer, grounded the same
conclusion in Genesis: political authority is the authority of fathers,
inherited from Adam's dominion, and no people was ever free to institute
anything. Hobbes has a chapter on paternal dominion, and it reads like a
deliberate reply: in [Chapter 20](/chapters/20-of-dominion-paternall-and-despoticall/)
dominion over children arises not from generation but rather from consent,
and in the state of nature it belongs to the *mother*, in whose power the
infant lies. Filmer returned the compliment in his *Observations* of 1652:
"I consent with him about the rights of exercising government, but I cannot
agree to his means of acquiring it." That sentence is the royalist reception
of *Leviathan* in miniature.

### The theory in court

England also litigated the question. Needing a fleet without a parliament,
Charles I levied <Gloss note="A levy for naval defence, traditionally
charged on coastal towns in emergencies, which Charles extended to the whole
kingdom from 1635 and collected annually — taxation without parliament in
all but name. John Hampden's refusal to pay twenty shillings brought the
test case.">ship money</Gloss>, and when John Hampden refused to pay, the
twelve judges of England heard, in effect, whether the king's absolute power
was law. He won, seven to five, in 1638, and the narrowness was the defeat:
the personal rule's finances and its theory failed together within two
years. Then in June 1642, weeks from war, the King's own printed *Answer* to
Parliament's Nineteen Propositions conceded that England was a mixed
monarchy of three estates, each balancing the others — the ablest royalist
pens buying moderate support at the price of the constitutional theory.
Hobbes never forgave the purchase. Mixed monarchy is, for him, the disease
[Chapter 29](/chapters/29-of-those-things-that-weaken-or-tend-to-the-dissolution-of-a-common-wealth/)
compares to a man with a second man growing out of his side; the King's
party had adopted the contradiction that was killing it.

### Absolutism from below

Hobbes's rebuilt version keeps the whole edifice and replaces the ground it
stands on. The sovereign's right comes from a covenant of every man with
every man; his person is [artificial](/concepts/person/), the
representative who bears the person of them all; and his power is absolute
precisely *because* it is authorised — whatever he does, each subject is
author of. The famous sentence keeps God in it, but as a courtesy of
rhetoric rather than a premise:

> This is the Generation of that great LEVIATHAN, or rather (to speake more
> reverently) of that Mortall God, to which wee owe under the Immortall God,
> our peace and defence.
>
> — [Chapter 17](/chapters/17-of-the-causes-generation-and-definition-of-a-common-wealth/)

And the construction is deliberately indifferent to how the power arrived.
A sovereign instituted by a frightened assembly and a conqueror submitted to
at swordpoint hold the same office by the same logic — "the Rights and
Consequences of both Paternall and Despoticall Dominion, are the very same
with those of a Soveraign by Institution" ([Chapter 20](/chapters/20-of-dominion-paternall-and-despoticall/))
— a symmetry that would shortly acquire a
[very practical application](/context/the-engagement-controversy/).

The royalists understood what had happened. A crown held from God cannot be
forfeited by failure; a crown held from the fear of subjects lasts, as
[Chapter 21](/chapters/21-of-the-liberty-of-subjects/) says plainly, no
longer than the power to protect them. Divine right could make a martyr of
a beaten king; Hobbes's theory could only make him a private man. In
January 1649 the scaffold outside the Banqueting House settled which theory
matched the facts, and *Leviathan*, two years later, offered England an
absolutism that no longer needed the man who had just died. An absolutism
built on consent, though, owed its readers a picture of what men without a
sovereign are actually like — and for that, Hobbes pointed across the
Atlantic. That evidence is [the next page](/context/america-and-the-plantations/).
