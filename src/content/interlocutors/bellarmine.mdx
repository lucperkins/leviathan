### Who he was

Roberto Bellarmino was born at Montepulciano in Tuscany, the nephew of a
pope, and entered the Society of Jesus at eighteen. He taught at Louvain
and then, for twelve years, held the chair of "controversies" at the Roman
College, the Jesuit university in Rome: the systematic defence of Catholic
doctrine against the Protestant reformers. The lectures became the
*Disputationes de Controversiis Christianae Fidei* (1586–93), three thick
volumes that for a century were the standard Catholic account of every
disputed question, and the standard target of every Protestant reply.
Clement VIII made him a cardinal in 1599, saying the Church had no equal to
him in learning. He served on the Inquisition, examined Giordano Bruno,
delivered the 1616 warning to Galileo, and wrote a catechism used across
Catholic Europe for three centuries. He was canonised in 1930 and declared a
Doctor of the Church the year after.

To English readers he was something more particular. When James I imposed an
Oath of Allegiance on his Catholic subjects after the Gunpowder Plot,
requiring them to deny that the Pope could depose the King, Bellarmine
answered the King in print, and James answered back, and the exchange drew in
the leading scholars of Europe. The question at issue, whether the Pope has
any authority, direct or indirect, over a sovereign in the sovereign's own
dominions, is the question of Part III of *Leviathan*. Bellarmine was the
name for the other side.

### De Summo Pontifice

The work Hobbes engages is the part of the *Controversies* on the Roman
pontiff, in five books. Hobbes introduces it, at the end of a long chapter
in which he has already argued his own position, as the strongest case that
can be made against him:

> because the Pope of Romes challenge to that Power universally, hath been
> maintained chiefly, and I think as strongly as is possible, by Cardinall
> Bellarmine, in his Controversie De Summo Pontifice; I have thought it
> necessary, as briefly as I can, to examine the grounds, and strength of
> his Discourse.

What follows is not a dismissal but a disputation, in the scholastic manner
Bellarmine himself used: Hobbes states each of Bellarmine's conclusions,
then each scriptural text or argument offered for it, then his answer. He
takes the five books in order.

**Book I: the government of the Church.** Bellarmine asks which form of
government is best, answers "mixed," and argues that the Church is a mixed
monarchy with St Peter at its head. Hobbes has already shown, he says, that
every government men are bound to obey is simple and absolute, so the first
conclusion falls; the second is "nothing to the question of the Popes Power
without his own Dominions"; and the text for the third, "Thou art Peter,
and upon this rock," proves only that the Church is founded on one article
of faith, that Jesus is the Christ.

**Book II: Peter at Rome.** That Peter was Bishop of Rome and the Popes his
successors Hobbes is prepared to grant. It gains nothing: if "Bishop of
Rome" means supreme pastor, then Constantine, not Silvester, was that
bishop, and every Christian sovereign is supreme pastor in his own
territory.

**Book III: Antichrist.** Hobbes declines the Protestant commonplace that
the Pope is Antichrist. He sees no proof of it in the sense Scripture gives
the word, and refuses to argue from it.

**Book IV: the Pope as supreme judge of faith and manners.** This is the
longest part of the reply. Bellarmine's four texts for papal infallibility
(Luke 22, Matthew 16, John 21, Exodus 28) are answered one by one; the
"Feed my sheep" of John 21 "contains no more but a Commission of
Teaching," and the Urim and Thummim were given to a High Priest who was
also the civil sovereign. Twelve texts for the Pope's power to make laws
are then taken in turn, and each is shown to concern counsel, not command:

> But who is there, that reading this Text, can say, this stile of the
> Apostles may not as properly be used in giving Counsell, as in making
> Laws? The stile of a Law is, We Command: But, We Think Good, is the
> ordinary stile of them, that but give Advice.

Finally, Bellarmine's seven arguments that bishops receive their
jurisdiction from the Pope are turned around: every one of them, Hobbes
says, proves instead that bishops receive it from their civil sovereign,
because "Jurisdiction is the Power of hearing and determining Causes
between man and man; and can belong to none, but him that hath the Power to
prescribe the Rules of Right and Wrong."

**Book V: temporal power.** Bellarmine concedes that the Pope is not lord of
the world, nor of Christendom, nor of any temporal jurisdiction *directly*
outside his own state, and claims only a supreme temporal power
*indirectly*, in order to the spiritual good. Hobbes grants the three
concessions and denies the fourth, "unlesse he mean by Indirectly, that he
has gotten it by Indirect means; then is that also granted." Six arguments
for the indirect power are examined. The central one, that the civil power
is subject to the spiritual, Hobbes dissolves by observing that
"Subjection, Command, Right, and Power are accidents, not of Powers, but of
Persons"; the last, from examples such as Ambrose excommunicating
Theodosius, gets the reply that "Examples prove nothing."

### Why Bellarmine, and why here

Bellarmine is the only modern author *Leviathan* argues with at length, and
Hobbes tells us why: the claim that a foreign power may command a sovereign's
subjects is, for him, the doctrine that dissolves common-wealths, and
Bellarmine had made it "as strongly as is possible." Answering him is
therefore the test of Hobbes's whole account of ecclesiastical power. The
answer has one shape throughout. Wherever Bellarmine finds a text that gives
authority to Peter, the Apostles, or the priesthood of Israel, Hobbes finds
that the authority is either the power to *teach*, which is not government,
or else the power of a *civil sovereign* who happened also to be high
priest. There is no third kind of power for the Pope to hold.

The argument does not end with the chapter. In Chapter 44, listing the
errors that make up the Kingdome of Darknesse, Hobbes refers back to "those
which Cardinall Bellarmine hath alledged, for the present Kingdome of God
administred by the Pope," and takes up the texts Bellarmine had offered for
purgatory.
