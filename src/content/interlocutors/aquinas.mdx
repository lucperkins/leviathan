### Who he was

Thomas was born near Aquino, between Rome and Naples, into a minor noble
family that intended him for a comfortable abbacy. He joined the new
Dominican order instead, studied under Albert the Great in Paris and
Cologne, and spent the rest of a short life teaching and writing, mostly in
Paris and Italy. His output was enormous: commentaries on Aristotle and on
scripture, disputed questions, the *Summa contra Gentiles*, and the
unfinished *Summa Theologiae*, a systematic account of God, creation,
human nature, morality, law, and grace that remains the most influential
work of Catholic theology ever written.

His achievement was a synthesis. Aristotle's works had arrived in the Latin
West a generation earlier, carrying a complete natural philosophy that owed
nothing to revelation and that many churchmen wanted banned. Thomas argued
instead that reason and faith could not truly conflict, and he rebuilt
Christian doctrine on Aristotelian foundations: matter and form, act and
potency, substance and accident, the four causes, the soul as the form of
the body, virtue as habit, and a moral order that reason can discover. By
the sixteenth century this was simply the philosophy of the schools. It is
what Hobbes was taught at Oxford, and what he meant when he wrote of "the
Schoole-men."

### The unnamed adversary

Hobbes does not mention Aquinas once in *Leviathan*. He names Aristotle
constantly, Suarez and Bellarmine when he wants a scholastic to quote, and
otherwise attacks "the Schooles" and their "Schoole-Divines" as a body.
There are reasons for the silence. Thomas was an authority for the Roman
Church, and Hobbes's quarrel in Part IV is with that Church's claim to power
rather than with any single theologian. And the scholasticism Hobbes knew
was late and Spanish, Suarez and his contemporaries, rather than the
thirteenth-century original. But the doctrines he takes apart are Thomas's,
and reading Hobbes against Thomas shows what the argument of *Leviathan* is
for.

### Against the schools' philosophy

Hobbes's first target is the vocabulary. In Chapter V, among the causes of
absurdity, he lists:

> The seventh, to names that signifie nothing; but are taken up, and
> learned by rote from the Schooles, as Hypostatical, Transubstantiate,
> Consubstantiate, Eternal-now, and the like canting of Schoole-men.

Every term on that list is one that Thomas made precise: the hypostatic
union of Christ's two natures, transubstantiation as a change of substance
under unchanged accidents, God's eternity as a single present. For Hobbes
they are not precise but rather empty, because the metaphysics that gives
them sense, of substances and accidents that can exist apart from bodies,
is itself empty. Chapter VIII makes the point by translating a chapter
title from Suarez and asking whether men who write whole volumes of such
stuff "are they not Mad, or intend to make others so?" The example he then
turns to is transubstantiation, the doctrine Thomas did most to define.

Underneath the ridicule is a real philosophical difference. For Thomas, the
world is intelligible because things have natures, forms that make them
what they are, and the mind knows by receiving those forms. For Hobbes,
there are only bodies in motion, and the mind knows by being pushed. The
Thomist doctrine of *species*, by which the form of a thing passes into the
eye and then into the intellect, is the first thing Hobbes attacks in the
book, in Chapter 1, as the teaching of "the Philosophy-schooles, through
all the Universities of Christendome."

### Against natural law as Thomas understood it

The deepest disagreement is about law, and here Hobbes keeps the words and
changes everything they mean. Thomas's natural law is a participation of
the rational creature in God's eternal law: a set of precepts that reason
discovers, that bind in conscience prior to any human command, and against
which human law can be measured. An unjust law, on this view, is not truly
a law at all.

Hobbes's laws of nature look similar, and he numbers them as the schoolmen
did. But in Chapter 15 he says what they really are:

> These dictates of Reason, men use to call by the name of Lawes; but
> improperly: for they are but Conclusions, or Theoremes concerning what
> conduceth to the conservation and defence of themselves; whereas Law,
> properly is the word of him, that by right hath command over others.

The laws of nature are theorems of prudence, not commands, and they become
law only when a sovereign commands them. That reverses Thomas's order. For
Thomas, natural law judges the civil law; for Hobbes, the civil law
determines what the natural law requires in practice, and the two "contain
each other, and are of equall extent," as Chapter 26 puts it. There is no
standard above the sovereign's law from which a subject could declare it
void. Hobbes's list of the causes of dissolution in Chapter 29 puts the
doctrine that private conscience can judge the law near the top; he is
naming, without naming him, the tradition of Thomas.

He even takes the schools' definition of justice, "the constant Will of
giving to every man his own," which is Thomas's from Justinian, and turns
it: where there is no property there is nothing that is one's own, and
there is no property until there is a commonwealth, so justice waits on the
sovereign rather than judging him.

### The soul and the church

Two further Thomist positions shape Parts III and IV. Thomas held that the
human soul is a subsistent form, naturally immortal, that survives the body
and awaits its resurrection. Hobbes denies that anything incorporeal exists
and argues, from scripture, that the dead are dead until the general
resurrection. And Thomas held that spiritual authority is superior to
temporal, so that the Church may direct rulers in matters touching
salvation. This is the position Hobbes finds elaborated in Cardinal
Bellarmine, a Thomist, and spends the longest chapter of the book refuting.
The claim that there is a "Kingdome of God" on earth, distinct from the
commonwealth and governed by priests, is for Hobbes the root of civil war,
and it is a claim the Thomist synthesis made systematic.

### What they share

It would be wrong to leave the two as simple opposites. Both are
systematic thinkers who believe morality can be reasoned out from human
nature. Both hold that the purpose of law is the common good and peace.
Both treat political authority as natural in the sense of being required
by what human beings are. The difference is in what they think human beings
are. Thomas's rational animal is ordered toward an end beyond itself, and
its laws are discoveries of that order. Hobbes's body in motion has no end
but its own continuation, and its laws are inventions for securing it.
*Leviathan* is, among other things, the *Summa* rewritten on the second
premise.
