Two speakers, A and B, go through the years from 1640 to 1660 in four
dialogues. Hobbes finished it around 1668, showed it to the King, and was
refused a licence to print — Charles II being unwilling to have his old tutor
explain in public whose fault the war had been. It circulated in manuscript,
was pirated after his death, and did not appear in an authorised text until
[Tönnies](/hobbes/impact/) edited it in 1889.

### The diagnosis

The title is the other monster of Job, and the pairing is deliberate: if
*Leviathan* is the commonwealth, Behemoth is the anarchy that comes when it
fails. The causes are given as a list of corrupted opinions, and the list is
recognisably
[Chapter 29](/chapters/29-of-those-things-that-weaken-or-tend-to-the-dissolution-of-a-common-wealth/)
with names attached: Presbyterian ministers preaching that subjects may judge
their sovereign; Catholics owing obedience to Rome; sectaries claiming private
inspiration; lawyers claiming a law above the King; the City of London rich
enough to think itself a republic; and, underneath all of it, the universities,
which he calls the core of the rebellion for teaching gentlemen to admire the
tyrannicides of Greece and Rome.

### What it adds

Two things. It is the only place where the argument is tested against events,
and it does not go easily: Hobbes has to explain why a sovereign with every
right he had claimed for one nevertheless lost, and the answer — that the King
had the right but not the means, having let others keep the militia, the pulpits,
and the schools — quietly concedes that rights without power are nothing, which
is his own doctrine turned on his own side.

It is also the best prose he ever wrote. The dialogue form lets him be funny,
and B's questions are genuinely obtuse in the way real questions are. Anyone
who finds *Leviathan* forbidding should read the second dialogue and see what
the same mind sounds like when it is not building a system.
