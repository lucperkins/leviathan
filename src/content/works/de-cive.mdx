In exile and expecting the war he had left, Hobbes took the political half of
the [Elements](/works/elements-of-law/) and rebuilt it in Latin. A hundred or
so copies went to friends in 1642; the public edition of 1647 added the long
defensive notes he had been forced to write in the meantime. It is the third
part of the system he had planned as *De Corpore* (*On Body*), *De Homine*
(*On Man*), *De Cive* (*On the Citizen*),
published first because, as he says, his country was boiling with the
questions it answers.

### The argument, in Latin

Three sections: liberty, dominion, religion. The state of nature is stated
more coldly than anywhere else — the famous phrase *bellum omnium contra
omnes*, the war of all against all, is from here, not from *Leviathan* —
and the laws of nature are laid out as theorems. The conclusion is the same
undivided sovereign, and the tone is that of a man writing for people who
will check the steps.

### Why it matters that it was Latin

It travelled. Continental readers who never opened *Leviathan* knew Hobbes
from this, and the objections that shaped the tradition were made to it:
[Pufendorf](/hobbes/impact/) built the natural law textbooks of Europe partly
out of it, [Spinoza](/hobbes/impact/) owned a copy and marked the exact point
of difference, and it is *De Cive* that Leibniz and Rousseau are usually
answering. The English version of 1651, *Philosophicall Rudiments Concerning
Government and Society*, arrived too late to matter beside the book printed
the same year.

### What *Leviathan* adds

Length, English, and God. *De Cive* handles religion in a third of its space
and handles it defensively; *Leviathan* devotes more than half the book to
scripture and goes on the attack, which is what made it the scandal and this
merely the standard text. It also lacks the doctrine of the
[person](/concepts/person/) and the artificial man of the frontispiece — the
images that make the later book memorable are exactly the parts *De Cive* does
without.
