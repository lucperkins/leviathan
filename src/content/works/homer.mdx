After 1666 he was effectively silenced in England on his own subjects: the
King's protection came with a condition, and
[Behemoth](/works/behemoth/) had been refused a licence. So he turned to
Homer, and between 1673 and 1676 published the *Odyssey* and then the *Iliad*
in English rhymed verse, complete. He was eighty-seven when the second
appeared.

### The verse

It is not good. He knew it, and got in first: the preface offers, as the
reason for the undertaking, that he had nothing else to do, and asks the
reader why he should trouble to be a better poet at that age. The lines are
cramped, the rhyme forces the sense, and Pope, forty years later, was not kind.
It has almost never been reprinted for its own sake.

### Why it is here anyway

Because of the preface, which is a real piece of criticism, and because of
what he chose. *Concerning the Vertues of an Heroique Poem* argues that the
merit of an epic lies in the clarity of its narration and the propriety of its
words, not in invention or fine sentiment — the same standard he applied to
philosophy, applied to poetry, by a man who forty-six years earlier had begun
his working life translating [Thucydides](/works/thucydides/) for the same
reason: that a plain account of how men behave under pressure teaches more than
any precept.

And because the choice of Homer is not neutral either. He had spent a lifetime
arguing that the classical authors taught Englishmen to love tyrannicide and
despise monarchy. Homer is the exception in his own account: an author of the
heroic, from before the schools, whose subject is what men do to each other
when there is no common power to keep them in awe. The last book he published
is about the state of nature.
