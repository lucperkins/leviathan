With *De Corpore* out, the middle book of the trilogy could be finished, and
in 1658, at seventy, he finished it. Nobody reads it. It has never had the
English translation the other two got in his lifetime, and the shape of it
explains why: about nine chapters on vision and optics, then six on man as an
animal that speaks, wants things, and can be governed.

### The optics

The long first half is a serious technical treatise on light, refraction, and
the eye, work he had been doing since the 1630s in Mersenne's circle and was
proud of. It matters to the system rather than to the subject. If sense is
motion in the organs — the claim [Chapter 1](/chapters/01-of-sense/) makes in
a page — then somebody has to show in detail how the motion gets from the
object to the eye and produces the appearance. This is the showing.

### The man

The later chapters are the useful ones, and they are more careful than
*Leviathan*. Speech is treated as the faculty that makes both science and
sedition possible. Good and evil are again referred to the appetite of the
speaker, but with a distinction *Leviathan* leaves implicit: what is good for
me, what is good for the commonwealth, and what men call good because they
have been taught to. The passions are given a fuller anatomy, and there is a
short account of the difference between the manners of men in different
conditions of life that reads like a first draft of a sociology.

### Why it is worth knowing about

It closes the argument the trilogy was designed to make: from bodies in
motion, to a particular kind of body that can talk and wants things, to the
artificial body those creatures build to keep from killing each other. Anyone
who suspects the politics of not really resting on the physics has to deal
with this book, because it is where the join is supposed to be made — and it
is a fair question whether the join holds.
